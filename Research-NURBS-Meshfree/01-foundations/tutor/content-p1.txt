# Part 1 · Foundations: B-splines , NURBS and reproducing kernels Bahagian 1 · Asas: B-spline , NURBS dan reproducing kernel
How a CAD curve is built from a knot vector, how a meshfree shape function is built from scattered nodes, and the exact point where the two meet: reproducing-kernel functions with blossom vectors are the B-splines. The map of the research area and how to read its papers close the part.

[notes-n1] Notes 1. The research problem: exact geometry meets meshfree flexibility
A finite element mesh only approximates the geometry that a designer drew in CAD. The CAD model itself is made of NURBS: smooth, exact, and described by a handful of control points. Two families of methods try to close the gap between design and analysis, and this research area lives where they meet.
Isogeometric analysis (IGA) Use the NURBS basis that describes the CAD geometry as the basis of the analysis too (Hughes, Cottrell & Bazilevs, CMAME 194 (2005) 4135–4195). The geometry is exact at every refinement; the price is the tensor-product structure of the patches, which makes local refinement and complex topology awkward.
Meshfree methods Build the shape functions from scattered nodes and their supports, with no element connectivity: EFG (Belytschko, Lu & Gu 1994), RKPM (Liu, Jun & Zhang 1995), RPIM and others. Nodes can be added anywhere; the price is paid at the boundary (no Kronecker delta, so essential conditions need care), in the integration, and in the cost of each shape function.
## A map of the area
The primer follows the papers the group has studied from first principles. They answer four questions, one per part:
Part Question Papers
1 Can a reproducing kernel produce the B-splines exactly? Zhang & Wang (2017)
2 How do IGA and meshfree regions share one consistent approximation? Wang & Zhang (2014); Valizadeh et al. (2015); Shaw, Banerjee & Roy (2008)
3 What integration and boundary treatment keeps the method exact for polynomials? Chen, Hillman & Rüter (2013); Chi & Lin (2016)
4 What do NURBS-based meshless methods (N-RPIM, NDM) gain, and what must they prove? Mokhtaram et al. (2022); Rasin et al. (2025)
Three things to keep apart The geometry map (how parameters become points), the field approximation (how the unknown is interpolated) and the integration cells (where the quadrature points sit). IGA uses one basis for the first two; the methods of this area deliberately separate them, and most of the difficulties come from forgetting which is which.
Key points:
- IGA analyses with the CAD basis itself: exact geometry, rigid tensor-product structure.
- Meshfree methods build shape functions from nodes: flexible refinement, harder boundaries and integration.
- Keep geometry map, field approximation and integration cells apart.
Quick check (not graded): What does isogeometric analysis use as the basis of the analysis?

Explanation shown after answering: The NURBS basis of the CAD model, so the geometry is exact at every refinement.

[notes-n2] Notes 2. B-splines from a knot vector
A knot vector is a non-decreasing list of parameter values Ξ = {t_1, t_2, …, t_{n+p+1}}. It defines n basis functions of degree p. An open knot vector repeats its first and last values p + 1 times, so the curve starts and ends at its first and last control points. The intervals [t_i, t_{i+1}) of non-zero length are the knot spans .
## The Cox–de Boor recursion
Start with piecewise constants, one per knot span, and raise the degree one step at a time:
EQUATION (2.1): N_{I,0}(\xi)=\begin{cases}1, & t_I\le \xi< t_{I+1}\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
EQUATION (2.2): N_{I,p}(\xi)=\frac{\xi-t_I}{t_{I+p}-t_I}\,N_{I,p-1}(\xi)+\frac{t_{I+p+1}-\xi}{t_{I+p+1}-t_{I+1}}\,N_{I+1,p-1}(\xi)
A fraction with a zero denominator (a repeated knot) is taken as zero. Each degree-p function is a blend of two degree-(p − 1) neighbours, so N_{I,p} is non-zero only on [t_I, t_{I+p+1}): p + 1 knot spans.
The derivative follows from the same two neighbours, one degree lower:
EQUATION (2.3): \frac{dN_{I,p}}{d\xi}=\frac{p}{t_{I+p}-t_I}\,N_{I,p-1}(\xi)-\frac{p}{t_{I+p+1}-t_{I+1}}\,N_{I+1,p-1}(\xi)
## Properties used everywhere in this area
EQUATION (2.4): \sum_{I=1}^{n}N_{I,p}(\xi)=1,\qquad N_{I,p}(\xi)\ge 0
- Partition of unity and non-negativity (2.4): a convex blend of control points.
- Local support : on one knot span only p + 1 functions are non-zero.
- Continuity C^{p−m} across a knot repeated m times; C^∞ inside a span.
- Linear reproduction with the Greville abscissae (2.5): the B-splines can be read as functions attached to nodes ξ̄_I.
EQUATION (2.5): \bar\xi_I=\frac{t_{I+1}+t_{I+2}+\dots+t_{I+p}}{p},\qquad \sum_{I=1}^{n}N_{I,p}(\xi)\,\bar\xi_I=\xi
## Worked step · counting and the Greville points
Quadratic (p = 2), Ξ = {0, 0, 0, 0.5, 1, 1, 1}: seven knots, so n = 7 − 2 − 1 = 4 functions on two spans. The Greville points are ξ̄ = {0, 0.25, 0.75, 1}. At ξ = 0.75 (second span) the recursion gives N = {0, 0.125, 0.625, 0.25}; the sum is 1 and Σ N_I ξ̄_I = 0.75 = ξ. Example 1 shows every step.
Key points:
- Cox–de Boor: degree 0 on spans, then blend two neighbours per degree (2.1)–(2.2).
- n = (number of knots) − p − 1; p + 1 non-zero functions per span.
- Greville points make the B-splines reproduce ξ: they act as nodes.
Quick check (not graded): How many quadratic B-splines does Ξ = {0, 0, 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1, 1} define?
Options: 4 | 6 | 8
Explanation shown after answering: Nine knots, p = 2: n = 9 − 2 − 1 = 6.

[notes-n3] Notes 3. NURBS and the geometry map
Polynomials cannot draw a circle exactly. Giving each B-spline a positive weight λ_I and normalising makes the basis rational, and rational quadratics represent every conic section exactly.
EQUATION (3.1): R_I(\xi)=\frac{\lambda_I\,N_{I,p}(\xi)}{\sum_{J=1}^{n}\lambda_J\,N_{J,p}(\xi)}
EQUATION (3.2): \{x(\xi)\}=\sum_{I=1}^{n}R_I(\xi)\,\{P_I\}
With all weights equal, R_I = N_{I,p}. The R_I keep the partition of unity and the local support, but they no longer reproduce ξ with the Greville points — a fact that matters in Part 2, when a meshfree correction must make up what the rational functions miss.
## Worked step · the exact quarter circle
Quadratic, Ξ = {0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1}, control points (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1), weights 1, 1/√2, 1. At ξ = 0.5 the B-splines are ¼, ½, ¼; the weighted values ¼, 0.3536, ¼ sum to 0.8536, so R = {0.2929, 0.4142, 0.2929} and x = y = 0.7071: radius exactly 1. Without the weight (λ_2 = 1) the same point lands at (0.75, 0.75), radius 1.0607.
## Surfaces, the map and its Jacobian
A NURBS surface is the tensor product of two curve bases, R_{IJ}(ξ, η) = λ_{IJ} N_I(ξ) M_J(η) / W(ξ, η), and the geometry map takes the parameter square to the physical patch. Derivatives in x and y come through the Jacobian, written in the row convention used throughout the primer:
EQUATION (3.3): \begin{Bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x\\ \partial f/\partial y\end{Bmatrix}=[J]^{-1}\begin{Bmatrix}\partial f/\partial\xi\\ \partial f/\partial\eta\end{Bmatrix},\qquad [J]=\begin{bmatrix}\partial x/\partial\xi & \partial y/\partial\xi\\ \partial x/\partial\eta & \partial y/\partial\eta\end{bmatrix}
Where meshfree methods enter Every method of Parts 2–4 keeps the NURBS map (3.2)–(3.3) for the geometry. What changes is the field: some or all of the unknown is interpolated by meshfree functions instead of R_I, either in the parameter square or in physical space.
Key points:
- Weights make the basis rational; rational quadratics draw conics exactly.
- R_I keep partition of unity and local support but not linear reproduction.
- Derivatives in x, y: {∂f/∂x} = [J]^{−1}{∂f/∂ξ}, row convention.
Quick check (not graded): Why does an exact circle need NURBS rather than B-splines?
Options: Polynomial B-splines cannot represent conic sections exactly; rational weights can | B-splines are not smooth enough on a circle | NURBS need fewer control points than B-splines for any curve
Explanation shown after answering: A circle is a conic; only a rational parametrisation draws it exactly. Smoothness is not the issue.

[notes-n4] Notes 4. Reproducing kernel shape functions
A meshfree method writes the unknown as a sum over nodes, with no elements:
EQUATION (4.1): u^h(x)=\sum_{I=1}^{n}\Psi_I(x)\,\hat u_I
Each node I carries a kernel (window) W_I(x) of support radius a. The shape functions are required to reproduce every polynomial up to degree p — the reproducing conditions , with {H(x)} = {1, x, …, x^p}^T:
EQUATION (4.2): \sum_{I=1}^{n}\Psi_I(x)\,\{H(x_I)\}=\{H(x)\}
## Derivation, step by step
## Step 1 · correct the kernel by a polynomial
Take the kernel and multiply it by a polynomial in the node position, with coefficients {b(x)} still unknown:
EQUATION (4.3): \Psi_I(x)=\{H(x_I)\}^T\{b(x)\}\,W_I(x)
## Step 2 · impose the reproducing conditions
Substitute (4.3) into (4.2). The coefficients come out of the sum, which leaves a (p + 1) × (p + 1) moment matrix :
EQUATION (4.4): [M(x)]\{b(x)\}=\{H(x)\},\qquad [M(x)]=\sum_{J=1}^{n}\{H(x_J)\}\{H(x_J)\}^T W_J(x)
## Step 3 · solve and substitute back
Solve for {b(x)} and put it back into (4.3):
EQUATION (4.5): \Psi_I(x)=\{H(x_I)\}^T[M(x)]^{-1}\{H(x)\}\,W_I(x)
## Step 4 · check the simplest case
For p = 0, [M] = Σ W_J and Ψ_I = W_I / Σ W_J: the Shepard functions, a partition of unity. For p = 1 in 1D, one entry: M_{12} = Σ x_J W_J(x); Example 2 computes all of them at x = 0.4.
The kernel used in the primer is the cubic B-spline window, r = |x − x_I| / a:
EQUATION (4.6): w(r)=\begin{cases}\tfrac23-4r^2+4r^3, & 0\le r\le\tfrac12\\[2pt] \tfrac43-4r+4r^2-\tfrac43 r^3, & \tfrac12<r\le 1\\[2pt] 0, & r>1\end{cases}
Two facts that drive the rest of the area (1) [M(x)] must be invertible: at least p + 1 kernels must overlap x, so the support radius a cannot be too small. (2) Ψ_I(x_J) ≠ δ_{IJ} in general: the functions do not interpolate, so essential boundary conditions need a transformation, Nitsche's method or a coupling with interpolating functions.
Writing {H(x − x_I)} instead of {H(x_I)} (a shifted basis) gives the same Ψ_I — the reproduced space is the same — but a much better conditioned [M]; scale by the support as well, {H((x − x_I)/a)}, and use it in code.
Key points:
- Reproducing conditions (4.2) + kernel-times-polynomial ansatz (4.3) ⇒ Ψ_I = {H(x_I)}^T[M]^{−1}{H(x)} W_I.
- [M] invertible needs p + 1 overlapping kernels: support size matters.
- No Kronecker delta: essential conditions need special treatment.
Quick check (not graded): Do RK shape functions satisfy Ψ_I(x_J) = δ_{IJ}?
Options: Yes, always | No, not in general, so essential conditions need special treatment | Only for p = 0
Explanation shown after answering: They reproduce polynomials but do not interpolate; Example 2 shows Ψ at a node is not 1.

[notes-n5] Notes 5. When RK becomes B-spline: blossom vectors
Compare (2.5) with (4.2): B-splines reproduce ξ with the Greville points in the role of node positions. They also reproduce ξ^2, …, ξ^p, but not with powers of ξ̄_I. The right node vectors are the blossom vectors (Zhang & Wang, CMAME 320 (2017) 474–508):
EQUATION (5.1): q_{I,l}=\frac{e_l(t_{I+1},\dots,t_{I+p})}{\binom{p}{l}},\qquad \{Q_I\}=\{1,\ q_{I,1},\ \dots,\ q_{I,p}\}^T
Here e_l is the elementary symmetric polynomial of the p interior knots of the support (e_1 = sum, e_p = product). q_{I,1} is the Greville point. With these vectors the B-splines satisfy reproducing conditions of exactly the form (4.2) — Marsden's identity:
EQUATION (5.2): \sum_{I=1}^{n}N_{I,p}(\xi)\,\{Q_I\}=\{H(\xi)\}
## The RK construction with blossom vectors
Repeat Section 4 with {Q_I} in place of {H(x_I)}, and give node I a kernel whose support is the support of N_{I,p}:
EQUATION (5.3): \Psi_I(\xi)=\{Q_I\}^T[M(\xi)]^{-1}\{H(\xi)\}\,W_I(\xi),\qquad [M(\xi)]=\sum_{J}\{Q_J\}\{Q_J\}^T W_J(\xi)
## Why the kernel cancels
On a knot span [t_k, t_{k+1}) exactly p + 1 supports are active, I = k − p, …, k. The reproducing conditions are p + 1 equations for these p + 1 values, and the vectors {Q_I} are linearly independent, so the system is square :
EQUATION (5.4): \left[\,\{Q_{k-p}\}\ \cdots\ \{Q_k\}\,\right]\begin{Bmatrix}\Psi_{k-p}\\ \vdots\\ \Psi_k\end{Bmatrix}=\{H(\xi)\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Psi_I(\xi)=N_{I,p}(\xi)
Its unique solution is the B-splines, by (5.2). Any positive kernel W_I leads to the same solution: (5.3) is just one way of solving (5.4). The kernel shape and the support radius inside the support no longer matter.
## Worked step · the blossom vectors of Example 1
For Ξ = {0, 0, 0, 0.5, 1, 1, 1}: {Q_1} = {1, 0, 0}, {Q_2} = {1, 0.25, 0}, {Q_3} = {1, 0.75, 0.5}, {Q_4} = {1, 1, 1}. At ξ = 0.75, Q_2, Q_3, Q_4 are active and the 3 × 3 system returns 0.125, 0.625, 0.25 — the B-splines of Example 1, for a constant, a cubic or a cosine kernel alike (Example 3).
What it buys, and what it does not Seen as RK functions, B-splines can be refined the meshfree way: insert nodes where needed and let the RK construction build the functions, while the square-system regions stay exact B-splines. The study of the 2017 paper found the catch: where nodes are inserted the system is no longer square, and linear exactness then depends on integration cells aligned with the knots — the theme of Part 3.
Key points:
- Blossom vectors (5.1) make the B-splines satisfy RK-type reproducing conditions (5.2).
- With B-spline supports the system is square on each span: Ψ_I = N_{I,p} for any positive kernel.
- Inserted nodes break the square system; integration then decides exactness.
Quick check (not graded): On one knot span of a degree-p space, how many blossom-RK functions are non-zero, and what does that make the reproducing system?
Options: p + 1; a square system with a unique solution | p; an underdetermined system | all n; an overdetermined system solved by least squares
Explanation shown after answering: p + 1 functions, p + 1 conditions: square, so the solution is the B-splines whatever the kernel.

[notes-n6] Notes 6. Reading the literature like a reproducer
Every paper of this area was studied by re-deriving it and repeating its figures. The same lessons came back in almost every one, and they are worth learning before your first literature review.
- Hidden parameters. The support convention (radius in what spacing?), the error norm and its quadrature, the reading of a boundary condition, the integration cells. A plot can depend on each of them by a factor of 2 to 60.
- The known-method limit. Check whether, for the paper's own settings, the method reduces to FEM, IGA or plain RK. One studied method was exactly Q1 FEM at its settings; another gained only 2–10 % over plain RK.
- The patch test first. A method that cannot reproduce a linear field exactly with its own quadrature has an integration problem, whatever its convergence plot shows.
- Bounded agreement. Write MATCH or NEAR with the tolerance (e.g. within 2 % or 5 %), name every choice the paper did not state, and run the literal reading next to the one that matches.
- What a result does not show. One mesh, one parameter set, one norm: say so. A research habit Before trusting a new method, implement the smallest case of it next to the method it claims to beat, with the same nodes, the same quadrature and the same boundary treatment. Most surprises in this area were found that way, in an afternoon.
Key points:
- List the unstated parameters before comparing numbers.
- Check the known-method limit and the patch test first.
- Report agreement with a tolerance, and what the result does not show.
Quick check (not graded): A paper reports “good agreement” but does not state the support size or the quadrature of its error norm. What should you do first when reproducing it?
Options: Use the usual default values and report a match | List the unstated parameters and run the literal reading and the plausible variants side by side | Tune the parameters until the plot matches, then report the match
Explanation shown after answering: Name every hidden choice and show what each reading gives; a fitted match is reported as a fit.

[glossary-knot-vector] Glossary: knot vector
knot vector: Non-decreasing list of parameter values that defines a B-spline basis.

[glossary-open-knot-vector] Glossary: open knot vector
open knot vector: A knot vector whose end values are repeated p + 1 times; the curve then interpolates its end control points.

[glossary-knot-span] Glossary: knot span
knot span: An interval [t_i , t_{i+1} ) of non-zero length; p + 1 basis functions are non-zero on it.

[glossary-b-spline-basis-function] Glossary: B-spline basis function
B-spline basis function: Piecewise polynomial N_{I,p} built by the Cox–de Boor recursion; non-negative, local, a partition of unity.

[glossary-cox-de-boor-recursion] Glossary: Cox–de Boor recursion
Cox–de Boor recursion: Builds N_{I,p} from two degree-(p − 1) neighbours, Eq. (2.2).

[glossary-partition-of-unity] Glossary: partition of unity
partition of unity: The functions sum to one at every point.

[glossary-local-support] Glossary: local support
local support: Each function is non-zero only on a small interval: p + 1 knot spans for a B-spline, radius a for a kernel.

[glossary-greville-abscissa] Glossary: Greville abscissa
Greville abscissa: Average of the p interior knots of a support, ξ̄_I ; with them the B-splines reproduce ξ.

[glossary-nurbs] Glossary: NURBS
NURBS: Non-uniform rational B-splines: weighted, normalised B-splines; exact for conic sections.

[glossary-weight] Glossary: weight
weight: Positive factor λ_I of a NURBS basis function; changes the shape without moving the control point.

[glossary-control-point] Glossary: control point
control point: Coefficient of a spline geometry; the curve lies in the convex hull of its control points.

[glossary-geometry-map] Glossary: geometry map
geometry map: The function x(ξ, η) that takes the parameter square to the physical patch.

[glossary-jacobian-row-convention] Glossary: Jacobian (row convention)
Jacobian (row convention): [J] with rows ∂(x, y)/∂ξ and ∂(x, y)/∂η, so that {∂f/∂x} = [J]^{−1} {∂f/∂ξ}.

[glossary-isogeometric-analysis-iga] Glossary: isogeometric analysis (IGA)
isogeometric analysis (IGA): Analysis with the NURBS basis of the CAD geometry.

[glossary-meshfree-method] Glossary: meshfree method
meshfree method: A method whose shape functions are built from nodes and supports, without elements.

[glossary-reproducing-kernel-rk] Glossary: reproducing kernel (RK)
reproducing kernel (RK): Shape function Ψ_I = kernel × polynomial correction that reproduces polynomials up to degree p.

[glossary-reproducing-conditions] Glossary: reproducing conditions
reproducing conditions: Σ Ψ_I (x){H(x_I )} = {H(x)}: polynomials up to degree p are returned exactly.

[glossary-moment-matrix] Glossary: moment matrix
moment matrix: [M(x)] = Σ {H(x_J )}{H(x_J )}^T W_J (x); must be invertible.

[glossary-kernel-window-function] Glossary: kernel (window) function
kernel (window) function: Non-negative bell-shaped function W_I centred on node I with support radius a.

[glossary-support-radius-dilation] Glossary: support radius · dilation
support radius · dilation: a = c_a h: the reach of a kernel, as a multiple c_a of a node spacing h.

[glossary-shepard-function] Glossary: Shepard function
Shepard function: The p = 0 RK function W_I /Σ W_J .

[glossary-shifted-basis] Glossary: shifted basis
shifted basis: {H(x − x_I )} instead of {H(x_I )}: same functions, better-conditioned [M].

[glossary-blossom-vector] Glossary: blossom vector
blossom vector: {Q_I } = {1, q_{I,1} , …, q_{I,p} }, built from the interior knots of a support, Eq. (5.1).

[glossary-marsden-s-identity] Glossary: Marsden's identity
Marsden's identity: Σ N_{I,p} (ξ){Q_I } = {H(ξ)}: B-splines reproduce polynomials with blossom vectors.

[glossary-elementary-symmetric-polynomial] Glossary: elementary symmetric polynomial
elementary symmetric polynomial: e_l : sum of all products of l distinct numbers from a list.

[glossary-essential-boundary-condition] Glossary: essential boundary condition
essential boundary condition: A prescribed value of the unknown; needs special treatment when Ψ_I (x_J ) ≠ δ_{IJ} .

[glossary-patch-test] Glossary: patch test
patch test: Check that a method reproduces a linear (or higher) field exactly with its own quadrature and boundary treatment.

[glossary-known-method-limit] Glossary: known-method limit
known-method limit: The settings at which a new method reduces to a known one (FEM, IGA, plain RK).

[glossary-hidden-parameter] Glossary: hidden parameter
hidden parameter: A choice a paper does not state but its results depend on (support convention, norm quadrature, BC reading).
