# ANSYS Demo
The two textbook problems rebuilt in ANSYS Workbench — predict, run, compare, explain: the heat plate and the cantilever, the mesh study, what a stress plot averages, four wrong models and their signatures, the first verified run of your project model, and how verification differs from validation in the hybrid project.

[start-outcomes] After this part you should be able to
1. Build a 2D steady-state thermal model and a 2D plane-stress model in ANSYS Workbench, with units, material, thickness, mesh, supports and loads entered correctly.
2. Map every ANSYS object to the hand calculation: element order, consistent loads, essential and natural conditions, averaged and element stresses, reactions.
3. Predict a result before running it, run a mesh study, and check the model against one independent calculation before reading any contour plot.
4. Distinguish verification from validation, recognise four classic modelling mistakes from their symptoms, and take a project model through the stage gates of the hybrid project.

[notes-n1] Notes 1. From the textbook to Workbench
Nothing in ANSYS Workbench is new mathematics. Its six cells are the six steps you have done with pen and paper: Engineering Data is [E] or k, Geometry is the domain, Mesh chooses the shape functions, Setup holds the loads and the essential conditions, Solution solves [K]{U} = {R}, and Results is [E][B]{û}. What is new is speed, and with speed the temptation to believe the first colour plot.

## Units first, then the five numbers of each problem
- Unit system: Metric (m, kg, N, s, V, A), set before anything else. Mechanical prints the unit beside every input field: read it.
- 2D: Analysis Type = 2D on the Geometry cell before the geometry is drawn; in Mechanical, 2D Behavior = Plane Stress and the Thickness (0.01 m for the cantilever).
- Material: the cantilever’s E = 2 × 10⁸ Pa and ν = 0.33 are the textbook’s, a thousand times softer than steel — keep them so that the numbers match the book. The plate’s k = 300 W m^{−1} K^{−1} (the §2.3 figure says 400: an erratum).

The most common failure in student projects A wrong unit system: geometry drawn in mm with loads in N and E in Pa is a model of a different structure, and nothing in the solver objects. A wrong unit shows up as a factor of 10³ or 10⁶ somewhere — which is why every result is predicted first.
Key points:
- Six Workbench cells = six hand steps; the software integrates and assembles, you decide the idealisation.
- q_y = −5 kN m^{−1} on a 2 m edge, t = 0.01 m: Force −10 000 N or Pressure −500 kPa; Pressure × edge area gives the Force back.
- Hand mesh: Edge Sizing 2 and 2, Linear. Plate 16 × 16: Element Size 0.0625 m.
Quick check (not graded): A load of 3 kN m^{−1} acts on a 1.5 m edge of a plate 0.02 m thick. The Pressure to enter is
Options: 150 kPa | 4.5 kPa | 0.06 kPa
Explanation shown after answering: p = q/t = 3/0.02 = 150 kN m^{−2} = 150 kPa. Check: 150 × (1.5 × 0.02) = 4.5 kN = q × 1.5, the total Force. 4.5 is that Force in kN, not a pressure; 0.06 is q × t.

## The independent check: heat in = heat out
EQUATION : [equation p6_heat: source not available in the page]
The reaction at a node with a prescribed temperature is the heat that must be removed there to hold it at 0 °C: the struck rows of [K]{T} − {R}, exactly as for the forces at a support. Their sum over both cold edges must equal what the body generates, on any mesh. Thickness does not change the temperatures; it scales every heat flow, so read the reaction with the model’s thickness in mind.
If the 2 × 2 run does not give 0.6214 Check the node count first: 9 nodes means the hand mesh, 25 means a 4 × 4 mesh (corner 0.5968). Then the element order: Program Controlled gives quadratic elements. Both are common, and both are found in a minute because the answer was predicted.
Key points:
- Linear 2 × 2: 0.6214 at the corner, 0.4821 at the mid-edges — the hand calculation, to four decimals.
- 16 × 16: 0.5898, +0.02% from the converged 0.5897; the hand mesh is +5.4%.
- Reaction on the cold edges = −Q A t: heat out equals heat generated on every mesh.
Quick check (not graded): Your “linear 2 × 2” plate run shows 0.5968 °C at the corner instead of 0.6214. The most likely cause is
Options: the mesh is 4 × 4, not 2 × 2 | the thickness is wrong | the heat generation was entered per unit area
Explanation shown after answering: 0.5968 is exactly the 4 × 4 linear value: an Element Size of 0.25 m instead of 0.5 m. Thickness cannot change a temperature in this problem; a source in the wrong unit would change it by a large factor, not by 4%. Check Mesh → Statistics: 25 nodes.

[notes-n3] Notes 3. Demo 2 · the cantilever and the mesh study
The second demonstration is the cantilever of Plane Stress Part 2: 4 m × 2 m, t = 0.01 m, fixed on the left, −10 kN on the right edge. Two things are predicted: the 2 × 2 linear run must give the hand value, 0.1306 m, and a refined quadratic mesh must approach Timoshenko beam theory, 0.1919 m.

At 1%: the 8 × 4 quadratic mesh, because the next refinement changed the deflection by only 0.35%; it is 0.7% below Timoshenko, the 16 × 8 0.35% below. Say it in one sentence with both numbers. Key points:
- Linear 2 × 2 = the hand calculation (0.1306 m) when the element is the textbook Q4; quadratic 2 × 1 already gives 0.1780 m.
- Quadratic study: changes 5.57%, 1.12%, 0.35%; report 8 × 4 or finer; Timoshenko 0.1919 m.
- R_y = +10 000 N and M = 40 000 N m on every mesh: the first number to read.
Quick check (not graded): A group’s quadratic study gives tip deflections whose changes are 5.6%, 1.1% and 0.35% (meshes 4 × 2, 8 × 4, 16 × 8). With a 1% tolerance they should report
Options: the 4 × 2 mesh, because the change from 2 × 1 is the largest | the 8 × 4 mesh (or finer): the next refinement changed it by 0.35% | only a mesh with zero change
Explanation shown after answering: The change attached to a mesh tells how much that refinement moved the result. After 8 × 4 the next step moved it 0.35%, below 1%, so 8 × 4 is converged to the tolerance; reporting 16 × 8 is also fine. A zero change never comes: each halving only shrinks the error.

[notes-n4] Notes 4. Stresses on screen, and four wrong models
A stress contour is the most persuasive and the least reliable picture Mechanical draws. Three questions from Plane Stress Part 3 decide what it means: what is being averaged, where the maximum sits, and whether that maximum converges.

[notes-n5] Notes 5. Your project model: the first verified run
The last block of the session is your own structure, from the model plan of Plane Stress Part 3. The aim is not a refined answer; it is a coarse model you can trust enough to refine. That takes eight checks, in this order, stopping at the first failure.
- Units and 2D. Metric (m, kg, N); Analysis Type 2D; plane stress or plane strain stated, with the reason.
- Geometry. Dimensions checked against the sketch; thickness entered.
- Material. E and ν (or k) with units, and where they came from.
- Supports. Only what is really restrained; nothing that is free is fixed.
- Loads. Tractions on edges and body forces; no forces on single vertices.
- Coarse mesh. Quadratic, a few elements; it must solve.
- Reaction. Equal to the applied load, with the right sign.
- Hand estimate. A deflection or a stress within a factor of 2 — then, and only then, refine. Steps 7 and 8 are the gate A model that fails step 7 or 8 is not refined; it is fixed. Refinement never repairs an idealisation: it converges, very precisely, to the answer of the wrong model.

- Common first-run problems: geometry in mm; thickness not set; a point force on a vertex; a whole edge fixed where the structure sits on a bearing; no restraint in one in-plane direction (rigid-body warning).

Key points:
- Eight checks in order; stop at the first failure.
- Reaction = load, and a hand estimate within a factor of 2, before any refinement.
- Report: number, check, signed difference, convergence — one sentence.
Quick check (not graded): Your coarse project model has the right reaction, but its deflection is 3.1 mm where your hand estimate is 0.9 mm. Next step?
Options: refine the mesh until the deflection settles | look for an idealisation or input error (supports, thickness, E, plane strain/stress) before refining | report 3.1 mm, since the software is more accurate than a hand estimate
Explanation shown after answering: A factor of 3.4 is outside the factor-of-2 gate. The reaction is right, so the loads are; the difference lies in stiffness or restraint — or in the hand estimate itself, which then needs fixing too. Refinement would converge to the same wrong model.

[notes-n6] Notes 6. Verification, validation and the hybrid project
Two different questions hide behind “is the model right?”. Asking them separately is what turns a colour plot into evidence.
Verification — solving the equations right Does the computer solve the mathematical model correctly? Checked against things that are exact for that model: a hand calculation on one element, a patch test, convergence to a known analytical solution, equilibrium of the reactions, an independent second code.
Validation — solving the right equations Does the mathematical model represent the real structure — plane stress or not, linear elastic or not, these supports or others? Only measurements can answer it: a load test, strain gauges, monitoring data. Without them, say plainly what the model cannot tell you.
The two demonstrations of this session were verification: ANSYS against hand calculations and textbook solutions. Agreement there says the software solves the idealised plate correctly; it cannot say that the idealisation was right — the four wrong models solved their own equations perfectly.
## The hybrid project: ANSYS first, your own tool as the independent check
In the project, ANSYS is the primary analysis. Each group also builds a small solver of its own — written with generative AI if you wish (“vibe coding”), deployed on GitHub — as an independent check of the ANSYS model. Two implementations that agree are stronger evidence than either alone; when they disagree, the difference is explained, never averaged away. The tool is only trusted after it passes the stage gates below, in order.
Gate What must be shown What it proves — and what it does not
0 Specification and validation plan, written before any code: the problem, the idealisation, the quantities of interest, the checks and their tolerances. That the group knows what “right” will mean. Nothing about the code yet.
1 Core solver: one element against a hand calculation (an entry of [k]); the patch test, exact to round-off; ΣR equal to the physical load. Element, assembly, loads and supports are consistent. Not that bending is right (Example 4).
2 Verification against a known solution: convergence to Timoshenko (cantilever) or Kirsch (plate with a hole, K_t → 3) with the observed order. The equations are solved right. Not that they are the right equations.
3 ANSYS cross-check on the project structure: same idealisation, comparable mesh; every difference above the tolerance explained (element formulation, load application, averaging). Two independent implementations agree. Not that the model matches the real structure.
4 UI and deployment: the tool runs from the group’s GitHub repository (GitHub Pages), with a README that states what it verifies and its limits. Others can run and check the tool.
5 Individual viva and demonstration: each member runs a gate live and explains the code and the numbers behind it. The understanding is the student’s, not the AI’s.
What the gates catch (Example 4) A load bug that gives every edge node σ_0tb instead of σ_0tb/2 still balances its own load vector: ΣR must be compared with the physical load, σ_0tH. A shear-modulus bug (Ē instead of Ē(1 − ν)/2) passes a tension patch test exactly, because the shear strain is zero there, and is caught only at Gate 2: the Q8 16 × 8 cantilever tip becomes 0.1696 m, 11.6% below Timoshenko.

[notes-n7] Notes 7. Workbench how-to: the steps students ask about
The demonstrations use rectangles. Your project structure will not: it has arcs, holes and fillets, loads that vary along an edge, and results you want as a curve rather than a colour plot. This section collects those steps for ANSYS Student, the free version downloaded from the Ansys website, which includes Workbench, DesignModeler, Discovery and Mechanical. The menu names were checked against the Ansys Help for release 2026 R1; if a menu sits elsewhere in your version, the object and its name are the same.
## “A curve in ANSYS”: four different things
Students ask “how do I draw a curve in ANSYS?” and mean one of four things. Decide which first; the steps are in different places.
You mean Where Step
a curved edge of the structure: an arc, a fillet, a hole, a spline Geometry: a DesignModeler sketch 2
a load that varies along an edge, such as water pressure on a wall Mechanical: Pressure → Magnitude → Function 4
a result plotted along a line: σ_x across the depth, T along an edge Mechanical: Construction Geometry → Path 5
a convergence curve: one result against the mesh your mesh-study table, plotted; or a Convergence object 5
## Step 1 · Open DesignModeler, not the default editor
- Toolbox → Static Structural (or Steady-State Thermal ), dragged onto the Project Schematic.
- Select the Geometry cell and, in its Properties, set Analysis Type = 2D . Do it now: it can be switched later, but switching may invalidate settings already made.
- Right-click the Geometry cell → New DesignModeler Geometry . A double-click opens the default editor, which in recent releases is Discovery; Surfaces From Sketches and the steps below are DesignModeler’s.
- In DesignModeler, choose the length unit (Units → Meter) before drawing, so that the dimensions match the numbers on this page.
## Step 2 · Draw a curved outline, a hole or a fillet
- Tree Outline → select XYPlane → New Sketch ; then the Sketching tab under the Tree Outline. Look At turns the view normal to the plane.
- Draw toolbox: Line or Polyline for straight edges; Arc by Center — click the centre, click the start point (it sets the radius), click the end point; Arc by 3 Points — click the start, the end, then a third point that sets the side and the radius; Arc by Tangent — an arc that leaves an edge tangentially; Circle for a hole; Spline — click at least three points, then right-click → Open End or Closed End.
- Modify toolbox: Fillet — type the radius in the Radius box beside the tool, then click the corner point or the two edges; right-click offers Trim Both, Trim 1st, Trim 2nd or Trim None. Trim removes the unwanted part of an edge where two curves cross.
- Dimensions toolbox: General, click an edge, type the value in the Details View. Constraints toolbox: Tangent, Concentric or Equal Radius when a shape must stay smooth or symmetric.
- Modeling tab → Concept → Surfaces From Sketches → select the sketch → Apply → Generate . The sketch must be closed and must not cross itself.
- A hole: draw the circle in the same sketch as the outer boundary; the inner loop becomes the hole. Look at the surface before going on — a filled circle means the loop was not recognised as a hole.
- A quarter model of a plate with a hole (Plane Stress Part 3): the two outer edges, the two cut edges and an Arc by Center at the corner; the symmetry conditions go on the cut edges in Step 4.
## Step 3 · Mesh a curved shape
- Mesh → Details: Element Order Linear or Quadratic. Quadratic elements follow a curved edge with curved sides; linear ones replace it with straight segments, so a hole needs more of them.
- Mesh → Insert → Sizing : on an edge, Type = Number of Divisions (the divisions round a hole set how well its stress peak is resolved); on the face, an Element Size.
- Face Meshing (mapped) works on four-sided faces; on a face with a hole, keep the free quadrilateral mesh or split the face.
- Generate, then read Mesh → Details → Statistics (nodes and elements). For the mesh study, change one sizing at a time and halve it each run.
## Step 4 · Supports and loads, including a load that varies along an edge
- Choose the Edge selection filter, select the edge(s), then Insert → Fixed Support, Displacement, Force or Pressure. A symmetry edge: Displacement with the normal component 0 and the other Free.
- Pressure, Define By = Normal To: a positive value pushes into the edge (compression). In 2D a Pressure acts on edges.
- A varying load: Magnitude → Function , then an expression that starts with “=” and uses x or y (one variable only). Set the unit system first: the function keeps the units active when Function was chosen. Example · water 5 m deep against a wall whose base is at y = 0 (m, N): the pressure is γ_w(5 − y), entered as =9810*(5-y) in Pa. Check after Solve: the horizontal reaction must equal ½ γ_w H² t = ½ × 9810 × 5² × t = 122.6 kN per metre of thickness.
## Step 5 · A result as a curve: probes, paths and convergence
- A value at a point: Solution → Insert → Probe (Deformation, or Force Reaction on a support).
- A result along a line: Model → Insert → Construction Geometry , then add a Path . Path Type = Two Points : Start and End coordinates (x, y, 0) and the Number of Sampling Points (up to 200, evenly spaced); or Path Type = Edge to follow an edge, a curved one included (the edge of a hole).
- Insert the result (for example Stress → Normal, Orientation X), set Scoping Method = Path , choose the path, and evaluate. The graph and its table appear under the plot; right-click the result → Export writes a tab-delimited text file that opens in Excel.
- A convergence curve: the course way is your own table of three or four meshes (§3), plotted against the number of elements. Mechanical can also refine for you: right-click a result → Insert → Convergence , set the Allowable Change (20% by default) and the Refinement Depth in the Solution details. Never converge on a singular point: its value grows without limit. Example · σ_x across the depth of the cantilever at x = 2 m: a Two Points path from (2, 0, 0) to (2, 2, 0) with 41 points, then Normal Stress X on that path. Beam theory predicts a straight line through zero at mid-depth: M = 20 kN·m, so σ_x = M(y − 1)/I = 1500 kN m^{−2} at y = 1.5 m (§4) and ±3000 kN m^{−2} at the top and bottom faces. A kink or an offset in the graph is a modelling error to explain.

[glossary-analysis-type-2d-2d-behavior] Glossary: Analysis Type 2D · 2D Behavior
Analysis Type 2D · 2D Behavior: Geometry-cell setting that makes the model two-dimensional; in Mechanical, the choice Plane Stress or Plane Strain, with a Thickness for plane stress.

[glossary-element-order] Glossary: Element Order
Element Order: Linear (4-node: PLANE182, thermal PLANE55) or Quadratic (8-node: PLANE183, thermal PLANE77); Program Controlled gives quadratic for 2D solids.

[glossary-edge-sizing-element-size-face-meshing] Glossary: Edge Sizing · Element Size · Face Meshing
Edge Sizing · Element Size · Face Meshing: Number of divisions on an edge, one target size for the whole face, and the request for a mapped (structured) quadrilateral mesh.

[glossary-enhanced-strain-element-control] Glossary: enhanced strain · Element Control
enhanced strain · Element Control: An option of the linear element that adds internal modes so it bends without locking; Element Control: Manual with full integration gives the textbook Q4.

[glossary-fixed-support-displacement] Glossary: Fixed Support · Displacement
Fixed Support · Displacement: Essential conditions: all dofs, or chosen components, of an edge set to zero or a value.

[glossary-force-pressure-on-an-edge] Glossary: Force · Pressure (on an edge)
Force · Pressure (on an edge): Natural conditions: a total force spread over the edge, or a force per unit area of the edge face; Mechanical turns either into consistent nodal loads.

[glossary-internal-heat-generation] Glossary: Internal Heat Generation
Internal Heat Generation: The source q_h in W m^{−3} on a body; with thickness t its total is q_h × area × t.

[glossary-reaction-probe] Glossary: Reaction probe
Reaction probe: Force Reaction, Moment Reaction or heat reaction on a support or prescribed temperature: the struck rows of [K]{U} − {R}. The first check of every run.

[glossary-averaged-unaveraged-elemental-mean] Glossary: Averaged · Unaveraged · Elemental Mean
Averaged · Unaveraged · Elemental Mean: Display Options of a stress result: nodal average, each element’s own nodal values, one centroid value per element.

[glossary-rigid-body-mode-weak-springs] Glossary: rigid-body mode · weak springs
rigid-body mode · weak springs: A motion the supports do not stop (here, translation in x); the solver warns and may add weak springs to solve anyway — the warning is the evidence.

[glossary-input-error-idealisation-error] Glossary: input error · idealisation error
input error · idealisation error: A wrong load, unit or support (shows in the reaction) against a wrong modelling choice that equilibrium cannot catch (shows only against a prediction).

[glossary-verification] Glossary: verification
verification: Solving the equations right: checks that are exact for the model — hand calculation, patch test, analytical solution, reactions, a second code.

[glossary-validation] Glossary: validation
validation: Solving the right equations: comparison of the model with measurements on the real structure.

[glossary-patch-test] Glossary: patch test
patch test: A small mesh of unequal elements under a load whose exact solution is a constant stress; a consistent element reproduces it to round-off.

[glossary-stage-gate] Glossary: stage gate
stage gate: A check that must be passed, with evidence, before the next stage of the project starts.

[glossary-observed-order-p] Glossary: observed order p
observed order p: The rate measured from three meshes: successive changes shrink by 2^p per halving of the element size.

[glossary-kirsch-solution] Glossary: Kirsch solution
Kirsch solution: The exact stresses around a circular hole in an infinite plate in tension: 3σ_0 at the edge of the hole (K_t = 3).

[glossary-designmodeler-discovery] Glossary: DesignModeler · Discovery
DesignModeler · Discovery: Two geometry editors opened from the Geometry cell: right-click → New DesignModeler Geometry for the sketch-based editor used on this page; a double-click opens the default editor, Discovery in recent releases.

[glossary-surfaces-from-sketches] Glossary: Surfaces From Sketches
Surfaces From Sketches: DesignModeler’s Concept command that turns a closed sketch on the XY plane into the surface body of a 2D model; an inner loop becomes a hole.

[glossary-path-construction-geometry] Glossary: Path (Construction Geometry)
Path (Construction Geometry): A line (Two Points) or an edge along which a result is sampled; a result with Scoping Method = Path gives a graph and a table.

[glossary-function-load] Glossary: Function load
Function load: A load magnitude given as an expression in x or y, starting with “=”, such as =9810*(5-y) for water pressure.
