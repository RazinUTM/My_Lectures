# Unit 1 Staircases I
SEAA 4333 Reinforced Concrete Design II, Week 1. Set a stair’s geometry by the by-laws, turn its waist into a load per m² on plan, model it as a beam on plan, then design and detail the waist as a one-way slab. Notes, slides, model answers, exercises with new numbers on every attempt, and the Staircase Lab.

[start-outcomes] After this part you should be able to
1. set the rise R, the going G and the number of risers for a storey, and check them against UBBL By-laws 106 and 108;
2. turn a waist thickness into a design load per m² on plan;
3. model a flight, with or without its landing, as a beam on plan, and find the total load F, the design moment M and the reactions;
4. design and detail the waist as a one-way slab, and find the slips in someone else’s calculation.

[notes-n1] Notes 1. A stair is a slab that climbs
Structurally, most stairs in buildings are slabs. The sloping slab under the steps is the waist; the steps sit on it as triangles of concrete that add weight but little strength. A stair that spans along its length carries its load from one support to the other like a one-way slab, so its design is the RCD I slab design with three new steps: the geometry, the load on plan and the span on plan.
[Figure: The parts of a stair. The waist h is measured square to the soffit; the effective span L is measured on plan, from the centre of support A to the centre of support B.]
Stair spanning along its length A stair carried by supports at its two ends: beams, walls or the edges of floor slabs. It is designed as a one-way slab on a strip 1 m wide.
- The waist h is measured square to the soffit, not vertically.
- The going G is the horizontal depth of a tread, without the nosing overhang.
- The rise R is the height of one step; the riser is the vertical face that makes it.
- The effective span L is measured on plan, centre to centre of the supports.
This week covers flights that span along their length, alone or with a landing at the top or the bottom. Week 2 adds stairs supported by their landings, two flights at right angles, and stairs with their sides built into walls, which span across their width.
Key points:
- A stair is designed as a one-way slab on a 1 m strip.
- Three steps are new: the geometry, the load on plan and the span on plan.
- h is measured square to the soffit; L is on plan, centre to centre.
Quick check (not graded): The waist h of a stair is measured
Options: vertically, from the soffit to the root of a step | square to the soffit | from the nosing down to the soffit | horizontally, along the going
Explanation shown after answering: h is the thickness of the slab itself, measured square to its soffit. The vertical thickness is larger, y = h/cos θ, and §3 uses it to find the load.

[notes-n2] Notes 2. Geometry and the by-laws
Three limits from the Uniform Building By-Laws 1984 (UBBL) and one comfort rule fix the geometry before any structural design starts.
Rule Limit Source
Rise R, the same on every step ≤ 180 mm UBBL By-law 106
Going G, the same on every step ≥ 255 mm UBBL By-law 106
Landing depth ≥ stair width UBBL By-law 106
Risers in one flight before a landing ≤ 16, buildings other than residential UBBL By-law 108
Comfort, 2R + G about 600 mm (550 to 700) Course notes
Comfort is not the law Comfort and legality are separate tests: a stair can pass one and fail the other. Some older notes print G ≤ 255 mm; By-law 106 sets a minimum, G ≥ 255 mm.
[Figure: Three stairs: A with R 170 and G 260, B with R 190 and G 240, C with R 175 and G 250 (mm). Which of them is legal? The first quick check below asks you.]
Quick check (not graded): Which of the three stairs in the figure is legal under UBBL?
Options: A only | A and C | All three | None of them
Explanation shown after answering: Only A: R = 170 ≤ 180 and G = 260 ≥ 255. B fails both (R = 190 > 180, G = 240 < 255). C is comfortable, 2R + G = 600 mm, but its 250 mm going is 5 mm short of 255: comfort does not make a stair legal.
## Setting the risers for a storey
- Least number of risers: n ≥ H/180, rounded up.
- Rise: R = H/n, which is now ≤ 180 mm.
- If n > 16, split the stair into two flights with a landing between them.
- Choose G ≥ 255 mm and check 2R + G against 550 to 700 mm.
- Remember the cost on plan: each extra riser adds one going to the length of the stair.
Quick check (not graded): Our school has a 3.5 m storey. What is the least number of risers, and in how many flights?
Options: 19 risers, one flight | 20 risers, two flights | 20 risers, one flight | 18 risers, two flights
Explanation shown after answering: n ≥ 3500/180 = 19.4, so n = 20, rounded up; R = 3500/20 = 175 mm ≤ 180. Since 20 > 16, use two flights of 10 with a half landing at 1.75 m. Rounding down to 19 gives R = 184 mm, which is illegal. With G = 260 mm, 2R + G = 610 mm.
Key points:
- R ≤ 180 mm and G ≥ 255 mm, uniform; at most 16 risers per flight; landing depth ≥ stair width.
- n = H/180 rounded up, never down.
- 2R + G ≈ 600 mm is comfort, not law.

[notes-n3] Notes 3. Average thickness and load on plan
A stair is designed as a beam on plan, so the sloping waist and its steps become one load per m² of plan area. Finishes and imposed loads are already given per m² on plan; only the self-weight of the flight needs converting.
[Figure: Section through a flight (WE 1.1: h = 125, R = 175, G = 260 mm). y is the vertical thickness of the waist; each step adds a triangle of average height R/2; t is the average vertical thickness.]
EQUATION (1.1): y = \frac{h\sqrt{R^2 + G^2}}{G}
EQUATION (1.2): t = y + \frac{R}{2}
EQUATION (1.3): g_k = 25\,t + g_{\mathrm{fin}}\ \ \text{(flight)}, \qquad g_k = 25\,h_L + g_{\mathrm{fin}}\ \ \text{(landing)}
EQUATION (1.4): n_d = 1.35\,g_k + 1.5\,q_k
- y = h/cos θ, where tan θ = R/G: the factor √(R² + G²)/G is 1/cos θ.
- Each step adds a triangle on its going whose average height is R/2, so t = y + R/2.
- Self-weight on plan is 25t kN/m² for a flight and 25h_L for a landing, with the thickness in m and concrete at 25 kN/m³. A landing is a flat slab, so it uses its own thickness.
Worked: WE 1.1 y = 125 × √(175² + 260²)/260 = 150.7 mm; t = 150.7 + 87.5 = 238.2 mm; g_k = 25 × 0.2382 + 1.20 = 7.155 kN/m²; n_d = 1.35 × 7.155 + 1.5 × 4.0 = 15.66 kN/m².
Quick check (not graded): The waist h = 150 mm and the going G = 260 mm stay the same; the rise R goes up from 150 to 180 mm, which is 20% more. By how much does the self-weight on plan rise?
Options: 0%: the waist did not change | About 10% | About 20%, like R | About 40%
Explanation shown after answering: R = 150: y = 173.2, t = 248.2 mm, 25t = 6.20 kN/m². R = 180: y = 182.4, t = 272.4 mm, 25t = 6.81 kN/m². That is +10%: R rose by 20%, but most of t is the waist y, which grew by only 5%; the steps add just R/2. Explorer tab 2 draws this curve.
Professional Habit 1, a preview A flight always carries more per m² on plan than a landing of the same thickness, because t > h. In this Unit’s examples the ratio is about 1.3. A ratio of 1.0 or less means a slip (§6).
Key points:
- y = h√(R² + G²)/G and t = y + R/2.
- Self-weight on plan: 25t for a flight, 25h_L for a landing (kN/m², thickness in m).
- n_d = 1.35g_k + 1.5q_k; the flight’s n_d is larger than the landing’s n_d.

[notes-n4] Notes 4. The stair as a beam on plan
Take a strip 1 m wide and treat it as a beam on plan, from the centre of one support to the centre of the other. A flight with a landing becomes a beam with two load segments: the flight’s n_d over the flight and the landing’s n_d over the landing.
[Figure: Faded Example 1.2 as a beam on plan: 17.69 kN/m over the flight (2725 mm) and 13.53 kN/m over the landing (1600 mm); A is continuous, B is simple. The full working is Example 2.]
Item Course rule
Span L Centre to centre of the supports, on plan, built from segments: each flight or landing plus half the width of the support at its end
Total load F F = Σ n_d × segment length, per metre width
Design moment M FL/10 when at least one end is continuous; FL/8 when both ends are simply supported
Hogging moment The same FL/10, over each continuous support
Reactions Statics, including the hogging moment at a continuous end
Deflection factor K 1.5 with both ends continuous; 1.3 with one end continuous (an end span); 1.0 with both ends simple (EC2 Table 7.4N)
EQUATION (1.5): L = \sum_i l_i \qquad\qquad F = \sum_i n_{d,i}\, l_i
EQUATION (1.6): M = \begin{cases} FL/10 & \text{at least one end continuous} \\ FL/8 & \text{both ends simply supported} \end{cases}
EQUATION (1.7): V_A = \frac{\sum_i F_i\,(L - x_i)}{L} + \frac{M_A - M_B}{L}, \qquad V_B = F - V_A
Here F_i is the load on segment i, x_i its centroid measured from A, and M_A, M_B the hogging moments at A and B, zero at a simple end. A continuous end therefore carries more shear than a simple one: in Faded Example 1.2 the hogging moment adds M_A/L = 30.21/4.325 = 6.98 kN to A’s reaction and takes the same amount from B’s.
Why FL/10 FL/10 is the course method (lecture notes; Mosley, Bungey and Hulse, §8.8). For an end span it is slightly conservative: BS 8110 gave 0.086FL near mid-span.
Quick check (not graded): A flight with its landing spans from a monolithic beam (A) to a brick wall (B). Which pair is right?
Options: M = FL/8, K = 1.0 | M = FL/10, K = 1.3 | M = FL/10, K = 1.5 | M = FL/12, K = 1.5
Explanation shown after answering: One continuous end gives M = FL/10 under the course method, and the end-span factor K = 1.3. C is the case with both ends continuous (K = 1.5); A treats the beam end as simple; D has no basis in the course method.
Key points:
- L is on plan, centre to centre of the supports, built from segments.
- M = FL/10 with a continuous end, FL/8 with none; K = 1.5, 1.3 or 1.0.
- A continuous end carries more shear: V_A includes (M_A − M_B)/L.

[notes-n5] Notes 5. Designing the waist
Once the stair is a beam on plan, the waist is designed as a one-way slab. The design runs in eleven steps; steps 3, 5 and 6 are new for stairs, and step 11 adds a stair’s own detailing.
Step What you do Reference
1 Design life, exposure class, fire resistance EN 1990 Table 2.1; EC2 Table 4.1 (RDF §1, §3, §5)
2 Materials: f_{ck}, f_{ctm}, f_{yk} EC2 Table 3.1
3 New: geometry and the average thickness t UBBL 106, 108; notes
4 Cover c_{nom} = c_{min} + Δc_{dev}, and the fire check EC2 4.4.1; EC2-1-2 Table 5.8
5 New: actions per m² on plan, g_k, q_k, n_d EN 1991-1-1; EN 1990 Eq. 6.10
6 New: analysis of a 1 m strip: L, F, M, V Notes; course method
7 Flexure: d, K, z, A_s, A_{s,min}, distribution bars RDF §6; EC2 9.2.1.1, 9.3.1.1
8 Shear: V_{Rd,c} ≥ V_{Ed} EC2 6.2.2; RDF §7
9 Deflection: (l/d)_{allow} ≥ L/d, with the right K EC2 7.4.2; RDF §8
10 Cracking: the bar spacing rules, for h ≤ 200 mm EC2 7.3.3, 9.3.1.1(3); RDF §9
11 Detailing: top bars, and the bottom bars at the kink Course detailing ; EC2 9.3.1.2(2)
EQUATION (1.8): K = \frac{M}{b d^2 f_{ck}} \le K_{bal} = 0.167, \qquad z = d\left[0.5 + \sqrt{0.25 - \frac{K}{1.134}}\right] \le 0.95\,d
EQUATION (1.9): A_s = \frac{M}{0.87\,f_{yk}\,z}, \qquad A_{s,min} = 0.26\,\frac{f_{ctm}}{f_{yk}}\,b d \ \ge\ 0.0013\,b d
EQUATION (1.10): V_{Rd,c} = 0.12\,k\,(100\,\rho_l f_{ck})^{1/3}\, b d \ \ge\ 0.035\,k^{3/2} f_{ck}^{1/2}\, b d, \qquad k = 1 + \sqrt{200/d} \le 2.0
EQUATION (1.11): \frac{l}{d} = K\left[11 + 1.5\sqrt{f_{ck}}\,\frac{\rho_0}{\rho} + 3.2\sqrt{f_{ck}}\left(\frac{\rho_0}{\rho} - 1\right)^{3/2}\right], \qquad \rho \le \rho_0 = \sqrt{f_{ck}} \times 10^{-3}
Equation (1.11) is EC2 Eq. 7.16a, used when ρ = A_{s,req}/bd does not exceed ρ_0, as in every stair of this Unit. The result is multiplied by A_{s,prov}/A_{s,req}, at most 1.5, and compared with the actual L/d.
## What a stair adds to the slab checks
Check What a stair adds Reference
Shear The continuous end carries more shear; take ρ_l from the top bars there EC2 6.2.2; RDF §7
Deflection K = 1.5 with both ends continuous, 1.3 with one, 1.0 with none; × A_{s,prov}/A_{s,req} ≤ 1.5 EC2 7.4.2, Table 7.4N; RDF §8
Main bar spacing ≤ min(2h, 250) mm where the moment is largest EC2 9.3.1.1(3)
Distribution bar spacing ≤ min(3h, 400) mm where the moment is largest EC2 9.3.1.1(3)
Distribution steel At least 20% of the main steel provided EC2 9.3.1.1(2)
Which spacing limits Some examples in the notes check spacing with the general limits, 3h ≤ 400 mm for main bars and 3.5h ≤ 450 mm for distribution bars. Where the moment is largest, which in a stair is mid-span and over a continuous support, EC2 tightens them to 2h ≤ 250 and 3h ≤ 400 (RDF §9). This course and the RC Design app use the tighter limits, which are never wrong.
Quick check (not graded): A waist is 175 mm thick. Where the moment is largest, what is the largest spacing allowed for the main bars?
Options: 525 mm (3h) | 400 mm | 350 mm (2h) | 250 mm
Explanation shown after answering: min(2h, 250) = min(350, 250) = 250 mm (EC2 9.3.1.1(3)). Faded Example 1.2 uses H10-150, well inside it. The general limit 3h ≤ 400 mm applies away from the peak moment.
Key points:
- Steps 7 to 11 are the RCD I slab design; steps 3, 5 and 6 are new.
- Shear: the continuous end governs. Deflection: K by the number of continuous ends.
- Spacing where M peaks: main ≤ min(2h, 250), distribution ≤ min(3h, 400) mm.

[notes-n6] Notes 6. Detailing, and the kink
Bottom bars carry the span. Top bars carry the hogging moment over a continuous support, and a simple support still gets some top steel against the partial fixity that a wall or a beam gives in practice.
Bars Rule Reference
01 Main bars, bottom Designed for M; spacing ≤ min(2h, 250) mm EC2 9.3.1.1(3)
02 Top bars, continuous end The same steel as 01, over 0.3L from the face of the support Course detailing
03 Top bars, simple end At least 25% of the span steel, over 0.2L from the face EC2 9.3.1.2(2)
04 Distribution bars At least 20% of 01; spacing ≤ min(3h, 400) mm EC2 9.3.1.1(2), (3)
[Figure: Faded Example 1.2 reinforcement: 01 main bars along the soffit, 02 top bars over 0.3L at the continuous end A, 03 top bars over 0.2L at the wall B. The detail at the kink is left for the quick check below.]
Where a flight meets its top landing, the soffit turns an inside (re-entrant) corner: the kink. A bar in the tension face cannot simply follow that corner. Decide which detail is right before you open the answer.
Quick check (not graded): Which bottom-bar detail is right at the kink?
Options: Detail A | Detail B
Explanation shown after answering: In A the bar is in tension on both sides of the bend. At an inside corner the two pulls add up to a force pointing out of the concrete, so the bar straightens and spalls the cover. In B the bars cross, and each runs on into the far face, where it is anchored in the compression zone. The same rule applies wherever a tension face turns an inside corner, for example where a retaining-wall stem meets its base (Week 14). [Figure: A, wrong: the bar pulls out and the cover spalls.] [Figure: B, right: the bars cross; each is anchored in the compression zone.]
## Professional Habit 1: sanity-check the load before you design
A flight carries more per m² on plan than its landing, because its average thickness t is greater than the landing’s thickness. A ratio of 1.0 or less means a slip.
Stair n_d flight n_d landing Ratio
WE 1.1, with a 125 mm landing for comparison 15.66 11.84 1.32
Faded Example 1.2 17.69 13.53 1.31
Stair S1 as submitted (Activity 1D) 12.15 12.15 1.00
In S1 this one glance catches slip A, the self-weight taken from the waist h. It cannot catch slip B, 1.35 on the imposed load, which lowers both loads alike; so check the partial factors line by line too.
Key points:
- Continuous end: top bars as the main bars over 0.3L. Simple end: at least 25% of the span steel over 0.2L.
- At the kink the bottom bars cross; each is anchored in the compression zone.
- Flight n_d ÷ landing n_d must exceed 1; check every partial factor as well.

[examples-x3] Example 3 · Explain-type questions
Exam type short written answers Time ≈ 3 min each
Questions
- Explain why a flight carries a larger load per m² on plan than a landing of the same thickness.
- A flight with its landing spans from a monolithic beam to a brick wall. State the design moment and the deflection factor K under the course method, and justify each.
- Why does the continuous end of that stair carry more shear than the simple end?
- Sketch and explain the correct bottom-bar detail where a flight meets its top landing.
Write your four answers first, then open the model answers one by one.
## Answer 1 The flight is thicker on plan
Measured vertically, which is how a load on plan sees it, the flight’s concrete is thicker than h for two reasons. The waist is y = h/cos θ = h√(R² + G²)/G, and each step adds a triangle of average height R/2, so t = y + R/2. With h = 175, R = 175 and G = 260 mm, t = 298.4 mm against a landing of 175 mm, and n_d = 17.69 against 13.53 kN/m², a ratio of 1.31.
## Answer 2 FL/10 and K = 1.3
M = FL/10, because one end is continuous with the beam: the course method uses FL/10 whenever at least one end is continuous, and FL/8 only when both ends are simply supported. K = 1.3, because the stair is an end span, continuous at one end only (EC2 Table 7.4N).
## Answer 3 The hogging moment shifts the reaction
The beam restrains rotation at A, so a hogging moment M_A develops there. Taking moments about B, A’s reaction gains M_A/L and B’s loses the same: V_A = ΣF_i(L − x_i)/L + M_A/L. In Faded Example 1.2, M_A/L = 6.98 kN, so V_A = 44.01 kN against V_B = 25.84 kN.
## Answer 4 Cross the bars at the kink
Do not bend a bottom bar round the inside corner: in tension, the pulls in its two legs add up to a force pointing out of the concrete, which straightens the bar and spalls the cover. Instead, run each set of bottom bars straight on past the corner into the opposite face, so that the two sets cross, and anchor each in the compression zone. Sketch: Notes §6, Detail B.

[glossary-flight] Glossary: flight
flight: An inclined run of steps between two levels or landings.

[glossary-landing] Glossary: landing
landing: A flat slab at the top or the bottom of a flight, or between two flights.

[glossary-waist-h] Glossary: waist h
waist h: The thickness of the inclined slab under the steps, measured square to the soffit.

[glossary-rise-r-riser] Glossary: rise R · riser
rise R · riser: R is the height of one step; the riser is the vertical face of the step. UBBL By-law 106: R ≤ 180 mm, the same on every step.

[glossary-going-g-tread] Glossary: going G · tread
going G · tread: G is the horizontal depth of a tread, without the nosing; the tread is the surface you step on. UBBL By-law 106: G ≥ 255 mm.

[glossary-nosing] Glossary: nosing
nosing: The front edge of a tread, which may overhang the riser below.

[glossary-soffit] Glossary: soffit
soffit: The underside of the waist or of the landing.

[glossary-pitch] Glossary: pitch θ
pitch θ: The slope of the flight: tan θ = R/G.

[glossary-vertical-waist-thickness-y] Glossary: vertical waist thickness y
vertical waist thickness y: The waist measured vertically: y = h/cos θ = h√(R² + G²)/G.

[glossary-average-thickness-t] Glossary: average thickness t
average thickness t: t = y + R/2: the mean vertical thickness of the waist and the steps, used for the self-weight on plan, 25t kN/m².

[glossary-load-on-plan] Glossary: load on plan
load on plan: A load given per m² of horizontal (plan) area. Stair loads and stair spans are both taken on plan.

[glossary-design-load-n-d] Glossary: design load n_d
design load n_d: n_d = 1.35g_k + 1.5q_k per m² on plan (EN 1990, Eq. 6.10).

[glossary-partial-factors-g-q] Glossary: partial factors γ_G , γ_Q
partial factors γ_G , γ_Q: 1.35 on permanent and 1.5 on variable actions at the ultimate limit state.

[glossary-effective-span-l] Glossary: effective span L
effective span L: The span of the beam on plan, centre to centre of the supports, built from segments: each flight or landing plus half the width of its support.

[glossary-one-way-slab-1-m-strip] Glossary: one-way slab · 1 m strip
one-way slab · 1 m strip: A slab that bends in one direction. A stair is designed on a strip 1 m wide, so its loads are per metre width and its steel per metre.

[glossary-continuous-end] Glossary: continuous end
continuous end: An end built monolithically into a beam or a slab that resists rotation, so a hogging moment develops there.

[glossary-simply-supported-end] Glossary: simply supported end
simply supported end: An end free to rotate, such as a slab resting on a brick wall; zero moment is assumed there.

[glossary-hogging-moment] Glossary: hogging moment
hogging moment: A moment that puts the top face in tension. Over a continuous support of a stair it is taken as FL/10.

[glossary-k-two-meanings] Glossary: K, two meanings
K, two meanings: In flexure, K = M/(bd²f_{ck} ), compared with K_{bal} = 0.167. In deflection, the structural factor K of EC2 Table 7.4N: 1.0 simply supported, 1.3 end span, 1.5 interior span.

[glossary-distribution-bars] Glossary: distribution bars
distribution bars: Secondary bars at right angles to the main bars: at least 20% of the main steel, at a spacing ≤ min(3h, 400) mm where the moment is largest.

[glossary-top-bars] Glossary: top bars
top bars: Bars near the top face over a support: the same as the main bars over 0.3L at a continuous end; at least 25% of the span steel over 0.2L at a simple end.

[glossary-kink] Glossary: kink
kink: The inside (re-entrant) corner of the soffit where a flight meets a landing. The bottom bars must cross there, not bend round it.

[glossary-ubbl] Glossary: UBBL
UBBL: Uniform Building By-Laws 1984. By-law 106: rise, going and landing depth; By-law 108: risers in one flight.

[glossary-comfort-rule-2r-g] Glossary: comfort rule 2R + G
comfort rule 2R + G: About 600 mm, from 550 to 700 mm (course notes). A comfort test, separate from the by-laws.

[glossary-professional-habit-1] Glossary: Professional Habit 1
Professional Habit 1: Sanity-check the load before you design: the flight’s n_d must exceed the landing’s n_d . A ratio of 1.0 or less means a slip.
