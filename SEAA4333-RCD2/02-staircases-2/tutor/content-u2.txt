# Unit 2 Staircases II
SEAA 4333 Reinforced Concrete Design II, Week 2. Unit 1 put a flight between two beams. Most stairs in a building rest on landings instead: an open-well stair lands on a slab that spans between two walls, a dog-leg stair shares its half landing with the next flight, and a stair in a core is built into the walls along its sides. This Unit follows the load of a flight to the ground in each case: it reads the stair plan, finds where the flight’s span ends inside a landing and which load lies on that span, designs the landing as a slab between the walls, shares a landing between two flights at right angles, and lets the walls carry the edges of a flight. Where does a flight’s load go when it rests on a landing? Notes, slides, model answers, exercises with new numbers on every attempt, and the Staircase Lab.

[start-outcomes] After this part you should be able to
1. read a stair plan: which way is up, how many risers and goings each flight has, the riser, the going and the plan length, and check them against the by-laws;
2. find the effective span of a flight that rests on a landing spanning between walls, put the load of the landing strip inside that span on it, and work out the moment and the reactions;
3. design the flight and the landing that carries it, the landing as a slab between the walls, with its bars, shear and deflection;
4. share a common landing between two flights at right angles, and allow for a flight built into walls along its sides.

[notes-n1] Notes 1. Where a flight’s load goes
Unit 1 put a flight between two beams and designed it as a one-way slab on plan. Real stairs are seldom that simple. A flight usually starts or ends on a landing, two flights often share one landing, and a stair inside a core of walls is cast into those walls. The flight is still a slab, and its design steps do not change; what changes is where its supports are, which loads lie between them, and which member carries what to the ground.
[Figure: Four ways to carry a flight. Unit 1 designed the first. This Unit follows the load in the other three: a landing spanning across, a landing shared by two flights at right angles, and walls along the sides.]
In an open-well stair the flights run side by side with a gap, the well, between them. Each flight rests at one end on a beam or a floor slab and at the other on a landing. The landing does not run along the flight: it spans across it, from one wall to the other, past the end of the well. So the flight is supported by the landing, and the landing is supported by the walls. This is Example 3 of the course notes, and it is the main case of this Unit.
When two flights meet at right angles , round two sides of a well, they share a quarter landing. The landing then lies inside the span of both flights at once, and its load is divided between them. This is Example 4 of the course notes. A flight built into walls along its sides hands a strip of its load straight to the walls, which BS 8110 allows the designer to use. Steps can also span across a flight between two stringer beams, or cantilever from a wall; each step is then a small beam (Mosley §8.8.1). Those two are named here and left to the RC Design app (cases ST9 and ST10).
Whatever the arrangement, the design of a stair answers three questions in order: where are the supports , which loads lie between them , and which member passes its load to which . The calculation of Unit 1 then runs unchanged. Most mistakes in stairs are made before that calculation starts, in one of those three answers.
Quick check (not graded): In an open-well stair, which member carries the reaction of a flight to the walls?
Options: The flight itself, spanning onto the wall | The beam at the other end of the flight | The landing, spanning between the walls | The well
Explanation shown after answering: The flight rests on the landing, and the landing spans across between the two walls. The landing is a member in its own right: it carries its own load and the reaction of the flight, and it needs its own design.
Quick check (not graded): Two flights meet at right angles at one quarter landing. Whose span is the landing part of?
Options: Neither: it is carried by its own beams | Both flights at once | Only the flight that goes up from it | Only the longer flight
Explanation shown after answering: Each flight spans through the landing to the support beyond it, so the landing lies inside both spans. That is why its load is shared between them (§5).
Key points:
- A flight is still a slab; what changes from Unit 1 is where its supports are and which loads lie on its span.
- Open well: the flight rests on a landing; the landing spans across between walls.
- Right angles: a common landing lies inside two spans; its load is shared.
- Built into walls: the walls take a strip of the load along each side.

[notes-n2] Notes 2. Reading a stair plan
Before any load, read the drawing. The course notes end their staircase chapter with the mistakes made in a past final examination, and none of them is a calculation: the dimensions of the stair, the number of steps, which way is up on the plan, and the wrong dimension used for the span. Each one starts a correct calculation on the wrong stair.
[Figure: Reading a plan. The arrows start at the floor of the plan: UP climbs the flight to the floor above, cut by the break line; DN descends to the half landing. Each flight has 10 risers but only 9 treads on its slope: the tenth step is the landing or the floor itself. R = 1700/10 = 170 mm; the plan length of the slope is 9 × 260 = 2340 mm.]
A stair plan shows the treads as parallel lines, the nosings of the steps. Arrows start at the floor the plan is drawn for: UP runs up the flight that climbs from that floor and DN runs down the flight that descends from it, so a DN arrow ends at the lower end of its flight; follow them to know which end of each flight is the bottom. Where the plan cut passes through the flight that climbs, a break line crosses it: the rest of that flight belongs to the plan of the floor above. Dimension strings give the treads as a count and a size, such as 9 @ 260 = 2340, and the widths of the landings and walls. The section, or a level on the plan, gives the heights.
Then count. The height a flight climbs is shared equally by its risers , so R = H_f/n_R. The plan length of its sloping slab is the number of goings on the slope times G. The two counts are not always equal. A flight that runs from one floor or landing to the next has one going fewer than risers, because its last step is the landing itself; that is the stair above, with 10 risers and 9 treads. The course notes’ Examples 3 and 4 draw 10 risers and 10 goings, because there the top going is drawn as part of the flight. Read the dimension string for the goings and the heights for the risers, and do not assume one from the other.
EQUATION (2.1): R = \frac{H_f}{n_R}, \qquad L_f = n_G\,G, \qquad n_G = n_R \ \text{or}\ n_R - 1\ \ (\text{read it from the plan})
Last, check the numbers against the rules of Unit 1: UBBL By-law 106 asks for R ≤ 180 mm and G ≥ 255 mm, the same on every step (one slide of the course notes prints “G ≤ 255”; the by-law sets a minimum), and a landing at least as deep as the stair is wide; By-law 108 allows at most 16 risers in one flight of a building other than a residence. 2R + G of about 600 mm (550 to 700) is the comfort rule of the notes, a separate test from the law. For the stair above: R = 170 ≤ 180, G = 260 ≥ 255, 2R + G = 600.
Quick check (not graded): A flight runs from a floor to a landing 1.53 m higher. Its plan shows 9 @ 250 = 2250, and the landing is the last step. How many risers does it have, and what is R?
Options: 9 risers, R = 170 mm | 10 risers, R = 153 mm | 11 risers, R = 139 mm | 8 risers, R = 191 mm
Explanation shown after answering: Nine treads on the slope and the landing as the tenth step: 10 risers, R = 1530/10 = 153 mm. Taking 9 risers would give 170 mm, a stair that does not fit the floor height.
Quick check (not graded): On a plan seen from the first floor, the arrow on a flight is marked DN. Where is the bottom of that flight?
Options: At the tail of the arrow | At the break line | It cannot be told from the plan | At the head of the arrow
Explanation shown after answering: DN points down the stair from the floor the plan is drawn for, so the arrow ends at the lower end. UP starts at the lower end. Either way, the arrow is the first thing to read.
Key points:
- Read the arrow first: UP starts at the bottom, DN ends at the bottom.
- R = H_f/n_R; L_f = n_GG. Count the goings from the dimension string; they may be one fewer than the risers.
- Check R ≤ 180, G ≥ 255, at most 16 risers, 2R + G near 600 mm.

[notes-n3] Notes 3. A flight on a landing: the effective span and its load
Here is the big question of the Unit. A flight rests on a landing that spans between two walls. The flight is a slab one metre wide spanning from its beam to the landing, but where exactly does its span end, and what load lies on it?
[Figure: The flight of the course notes’ Example 3. Its effective span runs from the centre of the beam to the centre of the landing: L = 2600 + ½(200 + 1500) = 3450 mm. The last 750 mm of the span is landing.]
Effective span of a flight supported by landings (BS 8110-1, 3.10.1.3; the course notes) L = L_a + ½(L_{b1} + L_{b2}), where L_a is the clear horizontal distance between the supports and L_{b1}, L_{b2} are the widths of the supports along the flight, a beam, a wall or a landing, each taken as not more than 1.8 m. EC2 has no rule of its own for stairs; the course uses this one.
EQUATION (2.2): L = L_a + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(L_{b1} + L_{b2}\right), \qquad L_{b} \le 1800\ \text{mm}
The support of the flight is therefore taken at the centre of the landing , up to 0.9 m into it. A landing is a wide, flexible support, not a knife edge. Mosley measures the span “between the centres of the supports” (§8.8.2), which for a landing is its centre; BS 8110 and the course notes add the 1.8 m limit for a very deep landing. For the notes’ Example 3: L_a = 10 × 260 = 2600 mm, L_{b1} = 200 mm (the beam), L_{b2} = 1500 mm (the landing), so L = 3450 mm.
Now the step that is easy to miss. The span ends 750 mm inside the landing, so the last 750 mm of the span is landing, and it carries the landing’s load. The total load on the span has two parts: the flight load over the flight, measured to the centre of the beam, and the landing load over the strip of landing inside the span.
EQUATION (2.3): F = n_f\,l_f + n_L\,l_L, \qquad l_f = L_a + \tfrac{1}{2}L_{b1}, \qquad l_L = \tfrac{1}{2}L_{b2}
With n_f = 15.0374 and n_L = 11.1825 kN/m² for the 150 mm flight and landing of Example 3: F = 15.0374 × 2.70 + 11.1825 × 0.75 = 40.601 + 8.387 = 48.988 kN per metre width. The course notes count the flight only, F = 15.04 × 2.7 = 40.6 kN: the 8.39 kN of landing inside the span, 17 % of the load, is missing (audit T-01 of the RC Design app). Every result that follows, the moment, the steel and the landing’s own load, inherits the shortfall.
The moment follows the end conditions, as in Unit 1: FL/10 when at least one end is continuous, here the beam at the foot, FL/8 when neither is. M = 48.988 × 3.45/10 = 16.901 kN m/m , against the notes’ 14.0. The reactions come from statics on the whole span, with the hogging moment at each continuous end (M_A, M_B; zero at a simple end). Taking moments about the beam end B, with each load at the centre of its own length and x_B measured from B:
EQUATION (2.4): M = \frac{FL}{10}\ \ (\text{an end continuous}) \ \text{or}\ \frac{FL}{8}; \qquad V_A\,L = \sum n\,l\,x_B + M_A - M_B, \qquad V_B = F - V_A
With M_A = 0 at the landing and M_B = 16.901 kN m at the beam: V_A = (8.387 × 3.075 + 40.601 × 1.35 − 16.901)/3.45 = (25.790 + 54.811 − 16.901)/3.45 = 18.464 kN/m at the landing end, and V_B = 48.988 − 18.464 = 30.524 kN/m at the beam. V_A is what each metre width of the flight puts on the landing: the starting point of §4.
[Figure: The load on the span, corrected: the flight over 2.70 m and the landing strip over 0.75 m. F = 48.99 kN, M = FL/10 = 16.90 kN m; V_B = 30.52 at the beam, V_A = 18.46 kN at the landing.]
Quick check (not graded): A flight has a clear horizontal length of 2400 mm between a beam 250 mm wide and a landing 2000 mm deep. What is its effective span?
Options: 3525 mm | 3425 mm | 2525 mm | 4650 mm
Explanation shown after answering: The landing counts as at most 1.8 m: L = 2400 + ½(250 + 1800) = 3425 mm. Without the limit you would get 3525 mm; stopping at the edge of the landing, 2525 mm, leaves out the part of the span that lies on the landing.
Quick check (not graded): In the same stair, which load lies on the last 900 mm of the span?
Options: None: the span carries only the flight | The flight load, as on the rest of the span | The landing load n_L, per m² on plan | The landing load over the whole 2 m
Explanation shown after answering: The span ends at the centre of the landing, 900 mm in (half of the 1.8 m limit), so its last 900 mm is landing and carries n_L, which is lighter than the flight load because a landing has no steps. Only the half inside the span counts.
Key points:
- L = L_a + ½(L_{b1} + L_{b2}), each L_b ≤ 1.8 m: the span ends at the centre of the landing.
- The landing strip inside the span carries n_L: F = n_fl_f + n_Ll_L. The notes’ Example 3 leaves it out (17 % of F).
- M = FL/10 with a continuous end, FL/8 without; reactions by statics with the hogging moment at each continuous end.
- V at the landing end is the load the flight puts on the landing.

[notes-n4] Notes 4. The landing: a slab between walls
Follow the load one member further. The flight puts V_A on the landing along the line where they meet. The landing spans across, between the two walls, so it is a one-way slab of its own: its breadth is the depth of the landing on plan, its span is the distance between the walls, and its supports are simple. It is designed exactly like a slab of Unit 1, for a load that the flight decides.
[Figure: The landing as a slab strip 1.5 m wide spanning 3.4 m between walls. Per metre of its span it carries its own load over its breadth and the reaction of the flights: w_L = 11.18 × 1.5 + 18.46 = 35.24 kN/m.]
Per metre of its span the landing carries two things: its own load n_L over its whole breadth b_L, and the reaction V_A of the flights that rest along it. The flight below the landing and the flight above it both bear on it, side by side, so the reaction runs along most of the span; the course notes and the RC Design app take it along the whole span, which ignores the narrow well and is slightly on the safe side.
EQUATION (2.5): w_L = n_L\,b_L + V_A \qquad [\text{kN/m along the landing}]
For the notes’ Example 3 with the corrected reaction of §3: w_L = 11.1825 × 1.5 + 18.464 = 16.774 + 18.464 = 35.238 kN/m . The notes, which left the landing strip out of the flight’s span, have 28.6 kN/m: the shortfall of §3 has travelled into the landing.
EQUATION (2.6): M_L = \frac{w_L L_L^2}{8}, \qquad V_L = \frac{w_L L_L}{2}
The landing is simply supported on the walls, with L_L = 3.4 m between their centres: M_L = 35.2375 × 3.4²/8 = 50.918 kN m and V_L = 35.2375 × 3.4/2 = 59.904 kN, both for the whole 1.5 m breadth (the notes: 41.3 kN m). Bending, shear and deflection are then checked on b = b_L = 1500 mm, with K = 1.0 for the span/depth ratio.
One detail sets the effective depth. Where the flight meets the landing, the flight’s main bars run into the landing in one direction and the landing’s main bars cross them in the other, so one set must lie on the other. The course places the landing bars in the inner layer, above the flight bars (RC Design app, decision D40), which is on the safe side; the notes take the outer layer, d = h_L − c − φ_L/2. Whichever is chosen, the drawing must show the same.
EQUATION (2.7): d_L = h_L - c - \phi_{\text{flight}} - \tfrac{1}{2}\phi_L \qquad (\text{landing bars in the inner layer})
d_L = 150 − 25 − 10 − 5 = 110 mm. K = 50.918 × 10⁶/(1500 × 110² × 25) = 0.1122, so z = 97.75 mm and A_s = 50.918 × 10⁶/(0.87 × 500 × 97.75) = 1197 mm² over the 1.5 m breadth. The notes’ 17H10 give 1335 mm² at (1500 − 2 × 25 − 10)/16 = 90 mm, so bending passes, and V_{Rd,c} = 107.9 kN ≥ 59.9 kN, so shear passes.
Deflection does not. ρ = 1197/(1500 × 110) = 0.73 % is above ρ_0 = 0.5 %, so Eq. 7.16b gives a basic ratio of only 16.17; with K = 1.0 and A_{s,prov}/A_{s,req} = 1335/1197 = 1.115 the limit is 18.0, against an actual 3400/110 = 30.9. The notes pass the landing at 28.7 ≥ 28.3 only because their lower moment and outer layer let the ratio of steel provided to steel required reach the cap of 1.5; with the corrected load the landing fails even with the outer layer (21.5 < 28.3).
A thicker landing is the cure; more bars only raise ρ and make Eq. 7.16b stricter. With h_L = 200 mm (d_L = 160 mm) the landing’s own load rises to n_L = 12.87 kN/m², and because the landing strip is part of the flight’s span, the flight changes too: F = 50.253 kN, M = 17.337 kN m and A_s = 349.6 mm²/m, so the flight needs H10-200 (393 mm²/m; H10-225 gives 349.1). The landing then carries w_L = 12.87 × 1.5 + 19.465 = 38.770 kN/m, so M_L = 56.02 kN m and A_s = 851 mm²: 12H10 (942 mm² at 131 mm) pass bending, shear (65.9 ≤ 123.3 kN) and deflection (28.5 ≥ 21.3). This is case ST7R of the RC Design app.
The landing’s main bars run between the walls, spaced at most min(2h_L, 250) mm where the moment is largest; its distribution bars, at least 20 % of the main steel provided, run in the direction of the flight. The flight’s bars continue into the landing and are anchored there.
Quick check (not graded): The notes’ landing: n_L = 11.18 kN/m², b_L = 1.5 m, span 3.4 m between walls; the flight’s reactions are 18.46 kN/m at the landing and 30.52 kN/m at the beam. What load does the landing carry per metre of its span?
Options: 16.77 kN/m | 35.24 kN/m | 47.29 kN/m | 56.48 kN/m
Explanation shown after answering: w_L = 11.18 × 1.5 + 18.46 = 35.24 kN/m: its own load over its breadth plus the reaction at the landing end. The 30.52 kN/m goes to the beam, not to the landing, and 11.18 × 3.4 would spread the landing’s load over its span instead of its breadth.
Quick check (not graded): Where do the main bars of the open-well landing go?
Options: Along the flight, as an extension of the flight bars | In the top face, over the walls only | Between the walls, in the bottom, in the layer above the flight bars | Diagonally, from the beam to the walls
Explanation shown after answering: The landing spans between the walls and sags, so its main bars run between the walls in the bottom. They cross the flight bars there; the course puts them in the inner layer, so d_L = h_L − c − φ_{flight} − φ_L/2.
Key points:
- The open-well landing is a simply supported one-way slab: breadth b_L, span L_L between the walls, K = 1.0.
- Its load per metre of span is w_L = n_Lb_L + V_A; M_L = w_LL_L²/8, V_L = w_LL_L/2.
- Landing bars in the inner layer: d_L = h_L − c − φ_{flight} − φ_L/2.
- The notes’ landing fails deflection with the corrected load; a 200 mm landing with 12H10 passes, and the flight then needs H10-200.

[notes-n5] Notes 5. Two flights at right angles
When two flights turn a corner round a well, the quarter landing at the corner belongs to both. Each flight spans from its beam, through the landing, to the wall on the far side of it. The landing therefore lies inside two spans at once, and both flights carry it.
[Figure: Two flights at right angles (the course notes’ Example 4). Each span runs from its beam through the common landing to the wall beyond it, so the landing’s load is shared: half of it in each span.]
Mosley §8.8.2 states the rule that the course notes use: loads common to two spans which intersect at right angles and surround an open well may be assumed to be divided equally between the spans. The landing is supported in two directions, by the two flights, and the equal split is the agreed simplification of that two-way load path; it is not a calculated share. In the span of each flight the landing therefore carries half of its load per m²:
EQUATION (2.8): n_{L}' = \tfrac{1}{2}\,n_L \qquad (\text{a landing common to two flights at right angles})
Example 4 of the notes: R = 170 mm, G = 255 mm, ten goings (2550 mm), h = h_L = 150 mm, g_k = 1.0 and q_k = 3.0 kN/m²; the flight rises from a 200 mm beam (continuous) through a 1.5 m landing to a 200 mm wall (simple). L = 2550 + 200/2 + 1500 + 200/2 = 4250 mm, of which 2.65 m is flight and 1.60 m landing. n_f = 14.8031 and n_L = 10.9125 kN/m², so the landing adds 10.9125/2 = 5.4563 kN/m²: F = 14.8031 × 2.65 + 5.4563 × 1.60 = 39.228 + 8.730 = 47.958 kN and M = FL/10 = 47.958 × 4.25/10 = 20.382 kN m . The reactions follow by statics as in §3: 33.44 kN/m at the beam, which carries the hogging moment, and 14.52 kN/m at the wall.
The halving is not a detail. Without it F = 56.69 kN and M = 24.09 kN m: A_s = 493 mm²/m, more than the notes’ H10-175 (449 mm²/m) provide, and the deflection check fails (25.8 < 35.4). With it A_s = 412 mm²/m, and H10-175 passes bending and deflection (38.0 ≥ 35.4). The notes ask “Load on landing ÷ 2. WHY?”: because the second flight carries the other half.
Halve only a landing that really lies in two spans at right angles. A landing at the end of a single straight flight is carried by that flight alone and keeps its full load, as in Unit 1. The landing of the open-well stair in §3 and §4 is not shared either: it is the support of the flights and spans onto the walls. And when the landing is halved in one flight, the other flight must be designed for the other half.
Quick check (not graded): Two flights at right angles share a quarter landing whose design load is 12.4 kN/m². What landing load enters the span of each flight?
Options: 12.4 kN/m² | 6.2 kN/m² | 3.1 kN/m² | 24.8 kN/m²
Explanation shown after answering: Half in each span: 12.4/2 = 6.2 kN/m², over the landing’s length in that span. Each flight carries half; together they carry all of it.
Quick check (not graded): In which of these is the landing load halved in the flight’s span?
Options: A straight flight with a landing at its top, carried by that flight alone | An open-well stair whose landing spans between two walls | Any landing deeper than 1.5 m | A quarter landing shared by two flights at right angles round a well
Explanation shown after answering: Only a landing common to two spans at right angles is shared. A landing carried by one flight keeps its full load; the open-well landing is a support, designed as a slab of its own (§4).
Key points:
- A landing common to two flights at right angles lies inside both spans: half of n_L in each (Mosley §8.8.2).
- The split is an agreed simplification of a two-way load path, not a calculated share.
- Example 4: F = 47.96 kN, M = 20.38 kN m, H10-175; unhalved, H10-175 would fail.
- Do not halve a landing carried by one flight, or the open-well landing that supports the flights.

[notes-n6] Notes 6. Flights built into walls
Inside a core of walls, a flight is often cast into the walls along its sides, at least 110 mm into a chase or poured with them. Near a wall the slab can pass its load sideways into the wall over a short distance, instead of carrying it along the span. BS 8110 turns this into a simple allowance, which the course uses; EC2 has no rule of its own for stairs.
Flight built into walls (BS 8110-1, 3.10.1.2; the course method) Where a flight is built at least 110 mm into a wall along its side and spans along the flight, a strip 150 mm wide next to the wall may be left out of the loaded area, and the effective breadth of the section may be increased by 75 mm. The course takes the strip and the 75 mm at each wall.
EQUATION (2.9): b_{ld} = b_F - 150\,n_w, \qquad b_{eff} = b_F + 75\,n_w, \qquad \kappa_w = \frac{b_{ld}}{b_{eff}}, \qquad n_f' = \kappa_w\,n_f
[Figure: Looking along the span of a flight built into walls on both sides. The 150 mm strip next to each wall carries no design load (b_{ld} = 900 mm) and the section resists over b_{eff} = 1350 mm: each metre of the effective breadth carries κ_w = 900/1350 = ⅔ of the load per m².]
The load on the loaded width b_{ld} is resisted by the effective breadth b_{eff}, so a 1 m design strip of b_{eff} carries κ_w times the load per m² on plan. F, M, the reactions and A_s are then per metre of b_{eff}, and the main bars are spaced across b_{eff}, into the chases.
Take Worked Example 1.1 of Unit 1: R = 175 mm, G = 260 mm, h = 125 mm, b_F = 1200 mm, both ends continuous on 250 mm beams, g_k = 1.2 and q_k = 4.0 kN/m², L = 2.85 m. Clear of walls it needed M = 12.72 kN m/m, A_s = 325 mm²/m and H10-225. Built into walls on both sides: b_{ld} = 1200 − 2 × 150 = 900 mm, b_{eff} = 1200 + 2 × 75 = 1350 mm and κ_w = 0.6667, so n′_f = 0.6667 × 15.6585 = 10.439 kN/m², F = 10.439 × 2.85 = 29.751 kN and M = FL/10 = 8.479 kN m per metre of b_{eff}, two thirds of the value clear of walls. With H8 bars (d = 96 mm) A_s = 214 mm²/m: H8-225 (223 mm²/m) passes every check, against H10-225 (349 mm²/m) without the walls. With a wall on one side only, κ_w = 1050/1275 = 0.824 and A_s = 267 mm²/m: H10-250.
Three conditions come with the rule. The flight must really be built into the wall, at least 110 mm, and the wall must be able to take the load. The rule applies to the flight and to a landing carried by that flight alone; a landing shared with a second flight borders the well, and the landing of an open-well stair spans onto the walls rather than along them, so both keep their full load. And the clause is read per wall: read as one strip and one 75 mm in all, κ_w with walls on both sides would be 0.824, the one-wall value, and Worked Example 1.1 would need more steel. The RC Design app applies the rule only when it is chosen under Design options; its default loads the whole width, which is conservative (decision D56).
Quick check (not graded): A flight 1500 mm wide is built into a wall on one side only. What is κ_w?
Options: 0.857 | 0.900 | 0.727 | 0.952
Explanation shown after answering: b_{ld} = 1500 − 150 = 1350 mm, b_{eff} = 1500 + 75 = 1575 mm, κ_w = 1350/1575 = 0.857. Leaving out the 75 mm gives 0.900; walls on both sides would give 1200/1650 = 0.727.
Quick check (not graded): Why may the 150 mm strip next to the wall be left out of the loaded area?
Options: Nobody walks within 150 mm of a wall | The strip is cracked and carries nothing | The wall carries the load on that strip directly, over a short distance | EC2 requires it for every stair
Explanation shown after answering: The load next to the wall goes sideways into the wall instead of along the span, so it does not load the 1 m design strip. People do stand there; the load is simply carried by another member. EC2 has no such rule; the course takes it from BS 8110.
Key points:
- Built into walls (≥ 110 mm): b_{ld} = b_F − 150n_w, b_{eff} = b_F + 75n_w, κ_w = b_{ld}/b_{eff}; design a 1 m strip of b_{eff} for κ_wn_f.
- Walls on both sides of Worked Example 1.1: M × ⅔, H10-225 becomes H8-225.
- Not for a shared landing or an open-well landing: they keep their full load.
- A course method from BS 8110; the RC Design app applies it only when chosen.

[examples-x3] Example 3 · Explain-type questions
Exam type short written answers Time ≈ 3 min each
Questions
- The support of a flight on a landing is taken at the centre of the landing, but never more than 0.9 m into it. Why the centre, and why the limit?
- In a stair with two flights at right angles, each flight is designed for half of the landing load. Has half of the landing load been lost?
- The landing of Worked Example 2.1 fails deflection, 18.0 against 30.9. A classmate suggests 20H10 instead of 17H10. Will it pass?
- Two students design the same 1200 mm flight, built into walls on both sides. One loads the whole width and provides H10-225; the other applies the BS 8110 rule and provides H8-225. Who is right?
Write your four answers first, then open the model answers one by one.
## Answer 1 A wide support, counted up to a point
A landing is not a knife edge: the flight hands its load to it over a width, and the landing itself bends. Taking the span to the centre of the support is the usual rule for a slab on a wide support; Mosley measures the span of a stair between the centres of its supports. A very deep landing would put that centre far from where the flight meets it, so BS 8110 counts the landing’s width only up to 1.8 m: at most 0.9 m of it lies inside the span. Inside that length the landing strip carries its own load, which belongs in F.
## Answer 2 No: the other flight carries it
Nothing is lost. The quarter landing lies inside two spans, one for each flight, so it is supported in two directions. Mosley §8.8.2 lets the load common to two spans at right angles be divided equally between them: each flight takes half, and together they take all of it. The split is an agreed simplification, not a calculated share. It holds only if the second flight is designed for its half; and it does not apply to a landing that one flight carries alone, nor to the open-well landing, which is a support.
## Answer 3 No: depth cures deflection, not bars
No. More bars raise A_{s,prov}/A_{s,req}, but that ratio is capped at 1.5, and the basic ratio stays at 16.17 because it depends on the steel required, which is already above ρ_0 = 0.5 %. The best that bars can give is 16.17 × 1.5 = 24.3, still below the actual 3400/110 = 30.9. The landing has to be deeper: at 200 mm, d_L = 160 mm, ρ falls to 0.35 %, the basic ratio rises to 25.8, and the actual ratio falls to 21.3. 12H10 then pass with room to spare.
## Answer 4 Both, on different assumptions
Both designs are safe. Loading the whole width ignores the help of the walls; it is conservative, and it is the RC Design app’s default. The BS 8110 rule, which the course uses, leaves a 150 mm strip next to each wall unloaded and adds 75 mm per wall to the breadth: κ_w = 900/1350 = ⅔, and H8-225 passes every check. The second answer is right only if its conditions are stated and met: the flight is built at least 110 mm into each wall, the walls can take the load, and the clause is read per wall (read as one strip in all, κ_w = 0.824 and more steel is needed). A design that uses an allowance must say so.

[glossary-open-well-stair] Glossary: open-well stair
open-well stair: Two flights side by side with a gap, the well, between them; each rests at one end on a landing that spans across between walls.

[glossary-well] Glossary: well
well: The open gap between the flights of a stair, seen on plan.

[glossary-dog-leg-stair] Glossary: dog-leg stair
dog-leg stair: Two flights that run in opposite directions with a half landing between them, and no well (or a very narrow one).

[glossary-half-landing-quarter-landing] Glossary: half landing · quarter landing
half landing · quarter landing: A landing where the stair turns through 180° (half) or 90° (quarter). A quarter landing is often shared by two flights at right angles.

[glossary-landing-as-a-support] Glossary: landing as a support
landing as a support: In an open-well stair the landing spans across between walls and carries the flight: a one-way slab of its own, simply supported, K = 1.0.

[glossary-effective-span-on-a-landing] Glossary: effective span on a landing
effective span on a landing: L = L_a + ½(L_{b1} + L_{b2} ), each L_b ≤ 1.8 m (BS 8110-1, 3.10.1.3): the span ends at the centre of the landing, at most 0.9 m into it.

[glossary-l-a] Glossary: L_a
L_a: The clear horizontal distance between the supports of a flight: its goings on plan.

[glossary-landing-strip-l-l] Glossary: landing strip, l_L
landing strip, l_L: The part of a landing that lies inside the flight’s effective span, ½L_{b2} . It carries the landing load n_L ; the course notes’ Example 3 leaves it out.

[glossary-reaction-on-the-landing-v-a] Glossary: reaction on the landing, V_A
reaction on the landing, V_A: The reaction of the flight at its landing end, per metre width of flight: the load the flight puts on the landing.

[glossary-w-l] Glossary: w_L
w_L: The load on the open-well landing per metre of its span: n_L b_L + V_A .

[glossary-inner-layer-outer-layer] Glossary: inner layer · outer layer
inner layer · outer layer: Where two sets of bars cross, one lies on the other. The course puts the landing bars in the inner layer, above the flight bars: d_L = h_L − c − φ_{flight} − φ_L /2.

[glossary-shared-landing] Glossary: shared landing
shared landing: A landing common to two flights at right angles round a well. It lies inside both spans, so each flight carries half of its load (Mosley §8.8.2).

[glossary-built-into-walls] Glossary: built into walls
built into walls: A flight cast at least 110 mm into the walls along its sides. BS 8110-1, 3.10.1.2, leaves a 150 mm strip at each wall unloaded and adds 75 mm per wall to the breadth.

[glossary-b-ld-b-eff-w] Glossary: b_{ld} , b_{eff} , κ_w
b_{ld} , b_{eff} , κ_w: Loaded width b_F − 150n_w ; effective breadth b_F + 75n_w ; κ_w = b_{ld} /b_{eff} , the share of the load per m² on a 1 m strip of b_{eff} .

[glossary-chase] Glossary: chase
chase: A groove left in a wall to receive the edge of a slab or a flight.

[glossary-stair-plan] Glossary: stair plan
stair plan: The drawing of a stair seen from above: the treads as parallel lines, an arrow from the bottom of a flight (UP) or to it (DN), a break line where the cut passes, dimension strings and levels.

[glossary-up-dn] Glossary: UP · DN
UP · DN: The arrow on a stair plan. UP starts at the lower end of the flight; DN, seen from the floor above, ends at it.

[glossary-break-line] Glossary: break line
break line: A zigzag line across a flight on plan where the horizontal cut passes; the rest of the flight is drawn on the plan of the floor above.

[glossary-risers-and-treads-n-r-n-g] Glossary: risers and treads, n_R , n_G
risers and treads, n_R , n_G: A flight that ends on a landing or a floor has one tread fewer on its slope than it has risers; read the treads from the dimension string and the risers from the heights.

[glossary-ubbl-by-laws-106-and-108] Glossary: UBBL By-laws 106 and 108
UBBL By-laws 106 and 108: R ≤ 180 mm and G ≥ 255 mm, the same on every step; a landing at least as deep as the stair is wide; at most 16 risers in a flight of a building other than a residence.

[glossary-0-and-eq-7-16b] Glossary: ρ_0 and Eq. 7.16b
ρ_0 and Eq. 7.16b: ρ_0 = √f_{ck} × 10⁻³. Above it the basic span/depth ratio falls to 11 + 1.5√f_{ck} ρ_0 /ρ: a heavily reinforced landing needs depth, not more bars.

[glossary-bs-8110-1-3-10] Glossary: BS 8110-1, 3.10
BS 8110-1, 3.10: The British code’s clauses for stairs that the course and the notes use where EC2 is silent: the effective span on landings (3.10.1.3) and flights built into walls (3.10.1.2).

[glossary-steps-spanning-across] Glossary: steps spanning across
steps spanning across: Steps carried across the flight between stringer beams or walls, or cantilevered from one wall: each step is a small beam (Mosley §8.8.1; app cases ST9 and ST10).

[glossary-professional-habit-2] Glossary: Professional Habit 2
Professional Habit 2: Draw the span, then load all of it: mark both supports on the drawing and check that every metre between them carries its load.
