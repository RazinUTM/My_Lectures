# Unit 14 Retaining Walls II
SEAA 4333 Reinforced Concrete Design II, Week 15, the last Unit. Unit 13 made the wall stand; this Unit makes it strong. A cantilever wall is three cantilevers joined at one corner: the stem bends towards the front, the toe bends up and the heel bends down, so each one cracks on a different face and takes its main bars there. The Unit finds the design loads on each part, sizes the steel in bending, checks shear and crack control, and details the bars: secondary steel, laps and curtailment. Then it asks what changes when the backfill slopes, when a key is cast under the base, and when the drains block and water rises behind the wall. Which face of each part is in tension? Notes, slides, model answers, exercises with new numbers on every attempt, and the Retaining Wall Lab.

[start-outcomes] After this part you should be able to
1. say which face of the stem, the toe and the heel is in tension, and find the design loads on each from the STR combination: the thrusts on the stem and the net pressures on the base;
2. design the stem, the toe and the heel in bending and check them in shear, with the minimum steel;
3. check crack control by the bar spacing and detail the wall: secondary steel, the lap of the starter bars and the curtailment of the stem bars;
4. allow for a sloping backfill, a shear key and water behind the wall, and say what each one changes.

[notes-n1] Notes 1. Three cantilevers: which face is in tension?
Unit 13 treated the wall as one rigid body and asked whether the ground could hold it. This Unit opens the body up. A cantilever wall is three slabs joined at one corner, and each of them is a cantilever: the stem stands up from the base, the toe reaches forward from the stem and the heel reaches back. Each one bends, and concrete cracks where it is stretched. Before any number, the designer has to see which face of each part stretches, because that is where the main bars go.
[Figure: A drawing comes back from the checker with one red circle. The stem bars are on the earth face, the toe bars in the bottom, the heel bars in the bottom. Which is wrong, and why?]
Take the parts one at a time and ask what pushes on each. The stem is pushed by the soil behind it, towards the front. It is held at its foot, so it bends like a diving board stood on end: the face against the earth stretches and the front face is compressed. Its main bars are vertical, close to the earth face .
The toe is pushed up by the ground: the bearing pressure is largest here. Only its own weight pushes down, because the soil in front may be dug away and is ignored. The toe bends upwards, like the edge of a pad footing: its bottom stretches.
The heel is the one that surprises. The ground pushes up on it too, but the backfill standing on it, the surcharge and its own weight push down harder: about 144 kN/m² down against 89 to 119 kN/m² up for the wall of Unit 13. The net load is downwards, the heel bends down like a balcony, and its top stretches. Bars in the bottom of the heel, as on the drawing above, would lie in the compression zone and do nothing.
[Figure: Three cantilevers, three tension faces: the earth face of the stem, the bottom of the toe, the top of the heel. The critical sections are at the root of the stem and at its two faces.]
Sketch first Draw each part as a line, fix it where it joins the others, draw the load on it as arrows, and let it bend. The main bars go on the outside of the curve. It takes ten seconds and it settles the question a formula cannot: a moment of 109 kN m/m says how much steel, not which face. Every drawing in this Unit starts this way.
Each cantilever has its largest moment and shear where it is fixed. For the stem that is its root, at the top of the base. For the toe and the heel it is the front and the back face of the stem. Those three sections are the ones designed; everywhere else the moment is smaller.
Quick check (not graded): In a cantilever wall with level backfill, the main bars of the heel are placed
Options: in the bottom, because the ground pushes the base up | at mid-depth, where the shear is largest | in the top, because the soil on the heel outweighs the bearing pressure | in both faces equally
Explanation shown after answering: The net load on the heel is downwards, so it sags and its top stretches. The toe is the part that bends up: its bars are in the bottom.
Quick check (not graded): The critical section for the bending of the stem is
Options: its root, at the top of the base | the underside of the base | mid-height of the stem | the ground level in front of the wall
Explanation shown after answering: The stem is a cantilever fixed into the base: moment and shear are largest at its root. The underside of the base was the bottom of the pressure diagram for stability, which is a different question (§3).
Key points:
- A cantilever wall is three cantilevers: stem, toe and heel, each fixed at the junction.
- Tension faces: the earth face of the stem, the bottom of the toe, the top of the heel.
- Sketch the deflected shape first; the main bars go on the outside of the curve.
- Critical sections: the root of the stem; the front and back faces of the stem.

[notes-n2] Notes 2. Design loads: the pressures under the base and the net loads
The stability checks asked whether each load helps or harms the wall as a body, and gave the weights 0.9 or 1.0. The design of the concrete asks a different question: what is the largest bending each part can see? That is the STR limit state, and its combination is the familiar one: 1.35 on every permanent action and 1.5 on every variable action, all acting together.
The weights and the earth thrust come from one source, the soil and the wall that holds it, so they take the same γ_G = 1.35 (EN 1990, 6.4.3.1(4)). The surcharge takes 1.5, both as a thrust and as a weight on the heel: here its weight is no longer favourable, because it bends the heel. The sums are those of the table of Unit 13; only the factors change:
EQUATION (14.1): N_d = 1.35\,\Sigma V_{k,G} + 1.5\,V_{k,Q}, \qquad \Delta M_d = 1.35\left(\Sigma M_{r,G} - M_{o,G}\right) + 1.5\left(M_{r,Q} - M_{o,Q}\right)
EQUATION (14.2): x_d = \frac{\Delta M_d}{N_d}, \qquad e_d = \frac{B}{2} - x_d, \qquad q_d = \frac{N_d}{B} \pm \frac{6N_d\,e_d}{B^2}
For the wall of Unit 13 as revised (toe 0.8 m, stem 0.4 m, heel 2.2 m, B = 3.4 m): ΣV_{k,G} = 258.1 kN and V_{k,Q} = 22.0 kN; ΣM_{r,G} = 527.75, M_{r,Q} = 50.6, M_{o,G} = 100.96 and M_{o,Q} = 32.53 kN m. Then N_d = 1.35 × 258.1 + 1.5 × 22.0 = 381.435 kN and ΔM_d = 1.35 × 426.79 + 1.5 × 18.07 = 603.27 kN m, so x_d = 1.58158 m, e_d = 1.7 − 1.58158 = 0.11842 m and q_d = 112.187 ± 23.444: 135.63 kN/m² at the toe and 88.74 kN/m² at the heel end.
[Figure: Design pressures (STR) and net loads on the base of Worked Example 14.1. The toe is pushed up; the heel is pushed down.]
The pressure falls in a straight line from the toe to the heel end, so its value at each face of the stem follows by proportion: 135.63 − (135.63 − 88.74) × 0.8/3.4 = 124.60 kN/m² at the front face and 88.74 + (135.63 − 88.74) × 2.2/3.4 = 119.08 kN/m² at the back face. Each part of the base then carries the difference between what pushes it up and what pushes it down:
EQUATION (14.3): n_{toe} = q_d - 1.35\,\gamma_c h \ \ (\text{upwards}), \qquad n_{heel} = 1.35\left(\gamma_c h + \gamma h_3\right) + 1.5\,w - q_d \ \ (\text{downwards})
The base slab weighs 1.35 × 25 × 0.4 = 13.5 kN/m². On the toe the net load is 135.63 − 13.5 = 122.1 kN/m² at its end and 124.60 − 13.5 = 111.1 kN/m² at the stem, upwards. On the heel the slab, the 4.5 m of backfill and the surcharge press down with 1.35 × (10 + 85.5) + 1.5 × 10 = 143.925 kN/m²; the net load is 143.925 − 119.08 = 24.8 kN/m² at the stem and 143.925 − 88.74 = 55.2 kN/m² at the heel end, downwards. Each net load is a trapezium, and its sign is the tension face of §1 turned into numbers.
The notes’ shortcut The course notes do not repeat the bearing calculation: they take the net pressures under service loads and multiply them by 1.35. That puts 1.35 on the surcharge instead of 1.5. For the notes’ own wall (heel 2.0 m) it gives 38.9 kN m/m in the toe and 102.5 in the heel, against 39.7 and 105.7 with the STR combination: 2 to 3 % lower. This page and the RC Design app use the STR combination; the app offers the notes’ method as an option, and either is accepted in Problem D14, where the design pressures are given.
Quick check (not graded): In the design of the heel, the weight of the surcharge standing on the heel takes the factor
Options: 0 | 1.0 | 1.35 | 1.5
Explanation shown after answering: Here the surcharge bends the heel: it is an unfavourable variable action, 1.5. In the sliding and overturning checks of Unit 13 the same weight helped and counted as zero. One load, a different factor for each question: Professional Habit 13.
Quick check (not graded): Under a base 3.0 m wide the design pressure is 140 kN/m² at the toe and 80 kN/m² at the heel end. The heel is 1.8 m long. The pressure at the back face of the stem is
Options: 104 kN/m² | 116 kN/m² | 110 kN/m² | 128 kN/m²
Explanation shown after answering: From the heel end the pressure rises by 60 kN/m² over 3.0 m: 80 + 60 × 1.8/3.0 = 116 kN/m². 104 measures the 1.8 m from the toe; 110 is the mean.
Key points:
- Members are designed for the STR combination: 1.35 on the weights and the soil thrust, 1.5 on the surcharge, thrust and weight alike.
- N_d and ΔM_d give x_d, e_d and the design pressures q_d = N_d/B ± 6N_de_d/B².
- Toe: q_d less the slab, upwards. Heel: slab, soil and surcharge less q_d, downwards.
- The notes multiply the net service pressures by 1.35: 2 to 3 % lower.

[notes-n3] Notes 3. The stem
The stem is a vertical cantilever one metre wide, fixed into the base and loaded by the active pressure on its own height. It is designed exactly as a slab: a moment and a shear per metre, a section 1000 mm wide and as deep as the stem is thick at its root.
Which height? (again) For stability the pressure diagram ran to the underside of the base, H = 4.9 m, because the whole body was being pushed. The stem is fixed at the top of the base, so only the pressure above that level bends it: h_s = h_3 = 4.5 m. Using 4.9 m for the stem overstates its moment by 26 %; using 4.5 m for stability understated the thrust by 16 %. Same wall, two questions, two heights.
EQUATION (14.4): P_{s,s} = \tfrac{1}{2}K_a\gamma h_s^2, \quad P_{q,s} = K_a w h_s; \qquad M_{Ed} = 1.35\,P_{s,s}\frac{h_s}{3} + 1.5\,P_{q,s}\frac{h_s}{2}, \quad V_{Ed} = 1.35\,P_{s,s} + 1.5\,P_{q,s}
[Figure: The stem of Worked Example 14.1. The moment grows with the cube of the depth for the soil and with its square for the surcharge: at mid-height it is one sixth of the moment at the root.]
For the wall of Unit 13 (K_a = 0.27099, γ = 19 kN/m³, w = 10 kN/m²): P_{s,s} = ½ × 0.27099 × 19 × 4.5² = 52.13 kN and P_{q,s} = 0.27099 × 10 × 4.5 = 12.19 kN per metre. At the root M_{Ed} = 1.35 × 52.13 × 1.5 + 1.5 × 12.19 × 2.25 = 105.6 + 41.1 = 146.7 kN m/m and V_{Ed} = 1.35 × 52.13 + 1.5 × 12.19 = 88.7 kN/m. The section is singly reinforced, as in a slab:
EQUATION (14.5): K = \frac{M_{Ed}}{b\,d^2 f_{ck}} \le 0.167, \qquad z = d\left[0.5 + \sqrt{0.25 - \frac{K}{1.134}}\,\right] \le 0.95d, \qquad A_s = \frac{M_{Ed}}{0.87 f_{yk}\,z}
EQUATION (14.6): A_{s,min} = 0.26\,\frac{f_{ctm}}{f_{yk}}\,b\,d \ \ge\ 0.0013\,b\,d
With a cover of 45 mm and H12 bars, d = 400 − 45 − 6 = 349 mm. K = 146.7 × 10⁶/(1000 × 349² × 30) = 0.040; the formula gives z = 0.963d, so the limit governs: z = 0.95 × 349 = 331.6 mm. A_s = 146.7 × 10⁶/(0.87 × 500 × 331.6) = 1017 mm²/m. For C30/37, f_{ctm} = 2.90 N/mm² and A_{s,min} = 0.001506 × 1000 × 349 = 526 mm²/m. Provide H12 at 100 (1131 mm²/m), vertical, 45 mm from the earth face.
A wall has no links, so the concrete alone must carry the shear:
EQUATION (14.7): V_{Rd,c} = 0.12\,k\left(100\rho_1 f_{ck}\right)^{1/3} b\,d \ \ge\ 0.035\,k^{3/2} f_{ck}^{1/2}\,b\,d, \qquad k = 1 + \sqrt{\frac{200}{d}} \le 2, \quad \rho_1 = \frac{A_s}{b\,d} \le 0.02
Here k = 1 + √(200/349) = 1.757 and ρ_1 = 1131/(1000 × 349) = 0.00324: V_{Rd,c} = 0.12 × 1.757 × (100 × 0.00324 × 30)^{1/3} × 349 = 157.0 kN/m, just above the lower bound of 155.8 kN/m. The shear of 88.7 kN/m is 56 % of it. If the shear did not pass, the cure would be a thicker stem, not links.
Quick check (not graded): A stem is 4.0 m high above a base 0.5 m thick; K_a = 0.30, γ = 18 kN/m³, no surcharge. The soil thrust that bends the stem is
Options: 54.7 kN/m | 43.2 kN/m | 21.6 kN/m | 86.4 kN/m
Explanation shown after answering: ½ × 0.30 × 18 × 4.0² = 43.2 kN/m, over the stem’s own height. 54.7 uses 4.5 m, the height for stability; 86.4 forgets the half.
Quick check (not graded): For a lightly loaded slab section the lever-arm formula gives z = 0.97d. The value used to find A_s is
Options: 0.97d | d | 0.95d | 0.82d
Explanation shown after answering: z ≤ 0.95d. In every member of this Unit K is small (0.01 to 0.05) and the limit governs, so z = 0.95d from the start: 331.6 mm for d = 349 mm.
Key points:
- The stem is bent by the pressure on its own height: h_s = h_3, to the top of the base.
- M_{Ed} = 1.35P_{s,s}h_s/3 + 1.5P_{q,s}h_s/2 at the root; V_{Ed} = 1.35P_{s,s} + 1.5P_{q,s}.
- Design as a slab 1 m wide: z ≤ 0.95d, A_s ≥ A_{s,min} = 0.001506bd for C30/37.
- No links in a wall: V_{Ed} ≤ V_{Rd,c}, or thicken the stem.

[notes-n4] Notes 4. The toe and the heel
The toe and the heel are horizontal cantilevers one metre wide, fixed at the faces of the stem. Each carries the trapezium of net load found in §2. A trapezium is a rectangle plus a triangle; here the larger intensity is at the free end of both, so the triangle has its peak there and its resultant lies two thirds of the way out.
EQUATION (14.8): M_{Ed} = n_f\,\frac{a^2}{2} + \left(n_e - n_f\right)\frac{a^2}{3}, \qquad V_{Ed} = \frac{n_f + n_e}{2}\,a
Here n_f is the net load at the face of the stem, n_e the net load at the free end and a the length of the cantilever. Toe (a = 0.8 m, upwards, 111.1 kN/m² at the stem and 122.1 kN/m² at the end): M_{Ed} = 111.1 × 0.8²/2 + 11.0 × 0.8²/3 = 35.55 + 2.35 = 37.9 kN m/m and V_{Ed} = ½ × (111.1 + 122.1) × 0.8 = 93.3 kN/m. With d = 349 mm and z = 0.95d, A_s = 37.9 × 10⁶/(0.87 × 500 × 331.6) = 263 mm²/m: half of the minimum, so A_{s,min} = 526 mm²/m governs. Provide H12 at 150 (754 mm²/m) in the bottom , as the notes do.
Heel (a = 2.2 m, downwards, 24.8 kN/m² at the stem and 55.2 kN/m² at the end): M_{Ed} = 24.8 × 2.2²/2 + 30.4 × 2.2²/3 = 60.02 + 49.05 = 109.1 kN m/m and V_{Ed} = ½ × (24.8 + 55.2) × 2.2 = 88.0 kN/m. A_s = 109.1 × 10⁶/(0.87 × 500 × 331.6) = 756 mm²/m. The notes’ H12 at 150 gives 754 mm²/m: short by a hair, now that the heel is 0.2 m longer than theirs. Provide H12 at 125 (905 mm²/m) in the top .
Shear is checked at the same faces with (14.7). With these light steel ratios the lower bound governs, 0.035 × 1.757^{3/2} × √30 × 349 = 155.8 kN/m, and both shears pass: 93.3 kN/m in the toe (60 %) and 88.0 kN/m in the heel (56 %). A base as thick as the stem root, the first-trial rule of Unit 13, usually leaves this margin.
Three parts, three answers Stem: 146.7 kN m/m, 1017 mm²/m, H12 at 100 on the earth face. Toe: 37.9 kN m/m, the minimum 526 mm²/m governs, H12 at 150 in the bottom. Heel: 109.1 kN m/m, 756 mm²/m, H12 at 125 in the top. The heel carries almost three times the moment of the toe although its net load is smaller, because it is nearly three times as long and the moment grows with the square of the length.
Quick check (not graded): A heel 2.4 m long carries a net downward load of 10 kN/m² at the stem, rising to 40 kN/m² at its end. The moment at the back face of the stem is
Options: 57.6 kN m/m | 86.4 kN m/m | 72.0 kN m/m | 115.2 kN m/m
Explanation shown after answering: 10 × 2.4²/2 + 30 × 2.4²/3 = 28.8 + 57.6 = 86.4 kN m/m. 57.6 uses a²/6 for the triangle, which puts its peak at the stem; 72.0 treats the load as uniform at 25 kN/m².
Quick check (not graded): A toe needs A_s = 263 mm²/m for its moment; A_{s,min} = 526 mm²/m. The steel to provide is at least
Options: 263 mm²/m | 395 mm²/m | 526 mm²/m | 789 mm²/m
Explanation shown after answering: The larger of the two: 526 mm²/m, H12 at 200 (565) or closer. A short toe is almost always governed by the minimum steel.
Key points:
- Toe and heel: cantilevers from the faces of the stem under a trapezium of net load.
- M_{Ed} = n_fa²/2 + (n_e − n_f)a²/3; V_{Ed} = ½(n_f + n_e)a.
- Toe: bars in the bottom, usually A_{s,min}. Heel: bars in the top, usually the larger moment of the two.
- Shear at the same faces, without links: V_{Ed} ≤ V_{Rd,c}.

[notes-n5] Notes 5. Crack control and detailing
Enough steel for the moment is not yet a finished wall. A retaining wall stands against wet soil for its whole life, so its cracks must stay fine; its bars must be spaced, lapped and stopped by rule; and the faces that carry no moment still need steel against shrinkage. This section finishes the drawing.
Crack control. For a member more than 200 mm thick EC2 limits the crack width to 0.3 mm. Without a crack-width calculation, that is done by limiting the bar spacing to the value of Table 7.3N for the steel stress under the quasi-permanent load (EC2 7.3.3):
EQUATION (14.9): \sigma_s = \frac{f_{yk}}{1.15}\times\frac{\text{quasi-permanent effect}}{\text{design effect}}\times\frac{A_{s,req}}{A_{s,prov}}
Steel stress σ_s (N/mm²) 160 200 240 280 320 360
Maximum bar spacing (mm), w_k = 0.3 mm 300 250 200 150 100 50
Between the columns of the table the spacing is interpolated. The ratio of the quasi-permanent to the design effect is found for each part from its own loads, with ψ_2 = 0.3 on the surcharge. For the stem it is a ratio of moments: M_{qp} = 52.13 × 1.5 + 0.3 × 12.19 × 2.25 = 86.4 kN m/m, so 86.4/146.7 = 0.589. For the toe and the heel the course takes the ratio of the vertical loads: (258.1 + 0.3 × 22.0)/381.4 = 0.694.
Stem, H12 at 100: σ_s = 434.8 × 0.589 × 1017/1131 = 230.3 N/mm²; the table gives 250 − 50 × 30.3/40 = 212 mm, and 100 mm is well inside. Heel, with the notes’ H12 at 150: σ_s = 434.8 × 0.694 × 756/754 = 302.6 N/mm²; the table gives 150 − 50 × 22.6/40 = 122 mm, so 150 mm is too wide. With H12 at 125 the stress falls to 434.8 × 0.694 × 756/905 = 252.1 N/mm² and the limit rises to 200 − 50 × 12.1/40 = 185 mm: passes. Toe: 434.8 × 0.694 × 263/754 = 105.3 N/mm², below the first column: 300 mm.
More steel, or closer steel? The crack check rewards both at once. Closing the heel bars from 150 to 125 mm adds 20 % of steel, which lowers the stress, and the lower stress allows a wider spacing than the one now provided. A bar that only just satisfies the moment (756 against 754 mm²/m) is working at its full stress under the design load and at about 300 N/mm² in service: it will usually fail the spacing check.
[Figure: The bars of Worked Example 14.1. Main bars on the three tension faces; distribution bars along the wall; the starter bars lap with the stem bars at the root, where the moment is largest.]
Secondary steel. Horizontal bars run along the wall, tied to the main bars. In the stem they must be at least a quarter of the vertical steel (EC2 9.6.3), and the notes provide not less than the minimum steel: max(0.25 × 1131, 526) = 526 mm²/m, H12 at 200 (565 mm²/m). The same distribution bars run along the base. The faces that are not in tension (the front of the stem, the top of the toe, the bottom of the heel) take a light mesh of the same order, against shrinkage and temperature cracks. Main bars are spaced at not more than 3h or 400 mm, secondary bars at not more than 400 mm.
Lap of the starter bars. The base is cast first, with starter bars standing out of it; the stem bars lap with them just above the base. That is the section of largest moment, and every bar is lapped there. EC2 8.7.3 gives l_0 = α_6l_{b,rqd} with α_6 = 1.5 when all the bars are lapped at one section and l_{b,rqd} = (φ/4)(σ_{sd}/f_{bd}). For C30/37, f_{bd} = 2.25 × 0.7 × 2.896/1.5 = 3.041 N/mm²; with the bars fully stressed, l_{b,rqd} = 3 × 434.8/3.041 = 428.9 mm and l_0 = 1.5 × 428.9 = 643 mm, 54 bar diameters. The course notes use 40φ = 480 mm, which is shorter: detail the lap from EC2.
Curtailment. The stem’s moment falls quickly with height: at mid-height it is 23.5 kN m/m, one sixth of the 146.7 kN m/m at the root. The notes stop every second stem bar at mid-height, 2.25 m above the base, leaving H12 at 200 (565 mm²/m) above. That is allowed on two conditions: the bars that continue still satisfy the minimum steel, and the bars that stop run an anchorage length past the level where they are no longer needed.
Quick check (not graded): A heel has σ_s = 260 N/mm² under the quasi-permanent load. Table 7.3N gives 200 mm at 240 N/mm² and 150 mm at 280 N/mm². The largest bar spacing allowed is
Options: 175 mm | 200 mm | 150 mm | 260 mm
Explanation shown after answering: Halfway between the two columns: 200 − 50 × 20/40 = 175 mm. Bars at 200 mm would fail; at 175 mm or closer they pass.
Quick check (not graded): The stem bars are H16 in C30/37 (f_{bd} = 3.04 N/mm²), all lapped with the starter bars at the root and fully stressed. The lap length by EC2 is about
Options: 480 mm | 572 mm | 640 mm | 858 mm
Explanation shown after answering: l_{b,rqd} = (16/4) × 434.8/3.04 = 572 mm; l_0 = 1.5 × 572 = 858 mm, 54φ. 640 mm is 40φ, the rule of thumb of the notes; 572 mm forgets α_6 = 1.5.
Key points:
- Crack control by spacing: σ_s = (f_{yk}/1.15) × ratio × A_{s,req}/A_{s,prov}, then Table 7.3N. Ratio: M_{qp}/M_{Ed} for the stem, N_{qp}/N_d for the base.
- Secondary steel: at least 25 % of the vertical steel and not less than A_{s,min}.
- Lap of the starter bars: l_0 = 1.5l_{b,rqd}, about 54φ in C30/37; 40φ is too short.
- Half the stem bars may stop at mid-height if the rest still give A_{s,min} and the stopped bars are anchored.

[notes-n6] Notes 6. A sloping backfill, the key and water
So far the backfill was level and drained, and friction alone held the wall. Three things change that picture. A backfill that slopes upwards pushes harder, and not horizontally. A key under the base adds resistance when friction is not enough. And water behind the wall, when the drains fail, is the commonest reason retaining walls move. Each one changes the loads; none changes the method.
## A sloping backfill
When the surface rises at an angle θ behind the wall, Rankine’s active pressure acts parallel to the surface and its coefficient depends on θ as well as φ:
EQUATION (14.10): K_a = \cos\theta\;\frac{\cos\theta - \sqrt{\cos^2\theta - \cos^2\varphi}}{\cos\theta + \sqrt{\cos^2\theta - \cos^2\varphi}}
EQUATION (14.11): P = \tfrac{1}{2}K_a\gamma H^2, \qquad P_h = P\cos\theta \ \ \text{at } \frac{H}{3}, \qquad P_v = P\sin\theta \ \ \text{at the heel end}; \qquad H = h_3 + a_2\tan\theta + h
[Figure: A backfill sloping at 15° (the course notes’ Example 2; app case RW2). The thrust has a vertical component, and it acts at the tip of the heel.]
The virtual back is taller than before, because the surface is higher above the heel end than above the stem: for the notes’ second wall (stem 4.5 m, base 4.0 × 0.4 m with a 2.8 m heel, θ = 15°, φ = 30°, γ = 16 kN/m³), H = 4.5 + 2.8 tan 15° + 0.4 = 5.6503 m. K_a = 0.37295 against 0.333 for a level surface, and P = ½ × 0.37295 × 16 × 5.6503² = 95.25 kN per metre: P_h = 92.0 kN and P_v = 24.65 kN. A wedge of soil, ½ × 16 × 0.75 × 2.8 = 16.8 kN, joins the table as well.
P_v is a vertical load on the virtual back, so in the stability table it helps: it adds to the friction and its restoring moment is 24.65 × 4.0 = 98.6 kN m. But the virtual back is the end of the heel. The same force is a line load on the tip of the heel cantilever: P_{v,d} = 1.35 × 24.65 = 33.28 kN at 2.8 m from the stem, 93.2 kN m/m by itself. With the net pressure and the wedge the heel moment is 168.7 kN m/m.
Counted once, forgotten once The course notes’ Example 2 counts P_v in the stability table and leaves it out of the heel. Their heel moment is 75.5 kN m/m and their bars are H12 at 150 (754 mm²/m). With P_v at the tip the moment is 168.7 kN m/m and the heel needs 1170 mm²/m: the bars provide 64 % of that. The bearing pressures under the heel already contain P_v, so a heel designed without it is not in equilibrium. The rule: every force in the table must land on one of the members. Faded Example 14.2 designs this heel.
The stem is bent by the horizontal part of the pressure on its own height: P_{s,s} = ½ × 0.37295 × 16 × 4.5² × cos 15° = 58.36 kN and M_{Ed} = 1.35 × 58.36 × 1.5 = 118.2 kN m/m. The notes take the diagram over the height of the virtual back above the base instead, 5.25 m, which gives 187.7 kN m/m: a convention on the safe side, 59 % heavier. The app uses the depth below the surface at the stem and offers the notes’ convention as an option.
## The key
When friction alone cannot hold the wall and the base cannot be lengthened, a key (Mosley: a heel beam) is cast under the base. To slide, the wall must now push the soil in front of the key out of the way, and soil resists being pushed far more than it pushes: the passive coefficient is the inverse of the active one.
EQUATION (14.12): K_p = \frac{1+\sin\varphi}{1-\sin\varphi} = \frac{1}{K_a}, \qquad H_p = \tfrac{1}{2}K_p\,\gamma\,a_k^2, \qquad H_d \le R_d + H_p
[Figure: A key instead of a longer heel (app case RW1K). Only the depth a_k below the underside of the base counts, and the front face must be cast against undisturbed ground.]
Only the depth a_k below the underside of the base is counted; the soil above it, in front of the toe, may be dug away. The factor on H_p is 1.0. For the notes’ wall, which slid in Combination 2 (100.2 against 95.6 kN), a key 0.6 m deep gives K_p = 3.69 and H_p = ½ × 3.69 × 19 × 0.6² = 12.6 kN. Combination 1: 103.4 ≤ 119.5 + 12.6 = 132.1 kN. Combination 2, with φ_d = 29.26°: K_{p,d} = 2.912, H_{p,2} = ½ × 2.912 × 19 × 0.6² = 10.0 kN and 100.2 ≤ 95.6 + 10.0 = 105.6 kN.
Passive pressure develops only as the soil is compressed, so a design should not lean on all of it. Here the check needs (100.2 − 95.6)/10.0 = 46 % of the passive force; a 0.5 m key would pass on paper while needing 67 %. The key itself is a short cantilever hanging under the base, pushed by the passive pressure: V_{Ed} = 1.35 × 12.6 = 17.0 kN/m, M_{Ed} = 17.0 × ⅔ × 0.6 = 6.8 kN m/m, tension on its front face. With 75 mm of cover against the soil, d = 319 mm and the minimum steel governs: H12 links at 200.
## Water behind the wall
Every wall in this course has a drained backfill: a layer of free-draining material behind the stem and weep holes through it. If the drains block, water stands in the backfill. Below the water table the soil is buoyed up and pushes with its submerged weight γ′ = γ_{sat} − γ_w only, but the water pushes with its full weight, and K_a does not apply to water:
EQUATION (14.13): p = K_a\left[\gamma z_w + \gamma'\,(z - z_w) + w\right] + \gamma_w\,(z - z_w) \quad \text{for } z > z_w, \qquad \gamma' = \gamma_{sat} - \gamma_w
[Figure: The same wall, drained and with the water table 1.5 m below the top (app case RW3). Below the water table each metre of depth adds 2.8 kN/m² of soil pressure and 9.8 kN/m² of water pressure.]
Take the revised wall of Unit 13, which passed every check, and let the water rise to 1.5 m below the surface (γ_{sat} = 20 kN/m³, so γ′ = 10.19 kN/m³). The soil thrust falls from 61.8 to 48.0 kN, but a water thrust of ½ × 9.81 × 3.4² = 56.7 kN joins it: 104.7 kN in all, 1.69 times the drained thrust. The water also lifts the base: an uplift of 56.7 kN takes the vertical load from 258.1 to 208.0 kN. Sliding in combination 1 becomes 161.3 against 104.0 kN: the wall whose friction was 25 % more than the sliding force now has a sliding force 55 % more than its friction, and the toe pressure rises from 98.6 to 123.8 kN/m².
To hold that water the heel would have to grow from 2.2 m to 4.0 m. A drain costs far less than 1.8 m of base, which is why the drainage is drawn, specified and inspected as carefully as the bars. Higher walls bring one more change, in the concrete itself: above 6 to 7 m, counterforts tie the stem to the heel, and both become slabs spanning between them instead of cantilevers. The RC Design app works such a wall (case RW4); its hand calculation is beyond this course.

Quick check (not graded): Below the water table, each extra metre of depth adds to the pressure on the wall (K_a = 0.271, γ′ = 10.19 kN/m³)
Options: 2.8 kN/m²: only the submerged soil | 9.8 kN/m²: only the water | 12.6 kN/m²: the submerged soil and the water | 5.1 kN/m²: as in the drained soil
Explanation shown after answering: 0.271 × 10.19 = 2.8 kN/m² from the soil plus 9.81 kN/m² from the water: 12.6 kN/m², against 0.271 × 19 = 5.1 kN/m² for the drained soil. That is why the thrust grows by 69 % in the example.
Key points:
- Sloping backfill: K_a from (14.10), H to the surface above the heel end, thrust at θ. P_v helps the stability and loads the heel tip.
- Key: H_p = ½K_pγa_k², factor 1.0, depth below the base only; do not rely on all of it.
- Water: submerged soil pressure plus full water pressure, and uplift under the base. Thrust × 1.69 in the example.
- Drainage is part of the design, not an accessory.

[examples-x3] Example 3 · Explain-type questions
Exam type short written answers Time ≈ 3 min each
Questions
- The ground pushes the whole base upwards. Why are the main bars of the heel in the top?
- For stability the pressure diagram was taken over 4.9 m; for the stem it is taken over 4.5 m. Why two heights for one wall?
- In Worked Example 14.1 the heel needs 756 mm²/m and H12 at 150 gives 754. Why would one more millimetre of bar not have settled it?
- A wall has stood for ten years and begins to lean after a wet season. Nothing was added to the backfill. What changed, and what would you look at first?
Write your four answers first, then open the model answers one by one.
## Answer 1 The net load decides the face
A slab bends towards the side it is pushed to, and what pushes the heel is the difference between the loads above it and the pressure below it. Above: its own weight, 4.5 m of backfill and the surcharge, 143.9 kN/m² at the design values. Below: the bearing pressure, 119.1 kN/m² at the stem falling to 88.7 kN/m² at the heel end. The difference is 24.8 to 55.2 kN/m² downwards, so the heel sags like a balcony and its top stretches. The toe has almost nothing above it, so there the ground wins and the bottom stretches. Sketch the net load first; the bars follow.
## Answer 2 Two questions, two free bodies
Each check has its own free body. For stability the body is the wall together with the soil on its heel, and the thrust acts on the back of that body, the virtual back, down to the underside of the base: 4.9 m. For the bending of the stem the body is the stem alone, cut at the top of the base where it is fixed, and only the pressure above the cut bends it: 4.5 m. Taking 4.5 m for stability loses 16 % of the thrust, on the unsafe side; taking 4.9 m for the stem adds 26 % to its moment, on the safe side but wasteful. Draw the free body, and the height is no longer a thing to remember.
## Answer 3 Strength is not the only limit
Because the area was not the only thing wrong with H12 at 150. A bar that only just satisfies the moment works at its full design stress, and under the quasi-permanent load at about 0.694 × 435 = 302 N/mm². At that stress Table 7.3N allows a spacing of 122 mm, and the bars are at 150 mm: the cracks would be too wide for a wall that stands against wet soil. Closing the bars to 125 mm cures both: 905 mm²/m is 20 % more than needed, the service stress falls to 252 N/mm², and the allowed spacing rises to 185 mm. In walls and slabs the spacing rule often governs the bars before the strength does.
## Answer 4 Water
Water. A wall is designed for a drained backfill; if the weep holes or the drain behind the stem block, water stands behind the wall after rain. Below the water table the soil pushes with less (its submerged weight), but the water pushes with all of its own, and K_a does not reduce it: each metre of depth adds 12.6 kN/m² instead of 5.1. For the wall of this Unit a water table 1.5 m below the top raises the thrust from 61.8 to 104.7 kN/m, lifts the base with 56.7 kN, and turns a sliding check with 25 % in hand into one that fails by 55 %. Look first at the drainage: are the weep holes running, is there staining or ponding behind the wall? Then at the base of the stem on the earth face, where the larger moment would crack it.

[glossary-anchorage-length] Glossary: anchorage length
anchorage length: The length a bar must run past a section to develop its stress by bond: l_{b,rqd} = (φ/4)(σ_{sd} /f_{bd} ).

[glossary-a-s-min] Glossary: A_{s,min}
A_{s,min}: The least tension steel in a section: 0.26(f_{ctm} /f_{yk} )bd, not less than 0.0013bd; 0.001506bd for C30/37. It usually governs a short toe.

[glossary-cover] Glossary: cover
cover: The concrete between a bar and the nearest face: 45 mm in the examples, 75 mm where the concrete is cast against soil, as in a key.

[glossary-crack-control] Glossary: crack control
crack control: Keeping the crack width within 0.3 mm. For members thicker than 200 mm, by limiting the bar spacing to Table 7.3N for the steel stress under the quasi-permanent load.

[glossary-critical-section] Glossary: critical section
critical section: The section where a cantilever is fixed and its moment and shear are largest: the root of the stem; the front face of the stem for the toe; the back face for the heel.

[glossary-curtailment] Glossary: curtailment
curtailment: Stopping bars where the moment no longer needs them. Every second stem bar may stop at mid-height if the rest still give A_{s,min} and the stopped bars are anchored.

[glossary-deflected-shape] Glossary: deflected shape
deflected shape: The exaggerated bent form of a member under its load. The tension face is the outside of the curve; sketch it before placing a bar.

[glossary-design-pressure-q-d] Glossary: design pressure, q_d
design pressure, q_d: The bearing pressure under the STR combination: q_d = N_d /B ± 6N_d e_d /B². It loads the toe and relieves the heel.

[glossary-distribution-steel] Glossary: distribution steel
distribution steel: See secondary steel.

[glossary-drainage] Glossary: drainage
drainage: The free-draining layer behind the stem and the weep holes through it, which keep water from standing in the backfill. Part of the design.

[glossary-effective-depth-d] Glossary: effective depth, d
effective depth, d: From the compression face to the centre of the tension bars: thickness − cover − φ/2; 349 mm for a 400 mm slab with H12 bars and 45 mm cover.

[glossary-heel-beam] Glossary: heel beam
heel beam: Mosley’s name for a key under the base. See key.

[glossary-k] Glossary: K
K: The normalised moment M_{Ed} /(bd²f_{ck} ); below 0.167 the section is singly reinforced. In walls it is small (0.01 to 0.05), so z = 0.95d.

[glossary-key] Glossary: key
key: A downstand cast under the base. The soil in front of it resists sliding passively: H_p = ½K_p γa_k ², over its depth below the base only.

[glossary-k-p] Glossary: K_p
K_p: Rankine’s coefficient of passive pressure, (1 + sin φ)/(1 − sin φ) = 1/K_a ; 3.69 for φ = 35°.

[glossary-lap-length-l-0] Glossary: lap length, l_0
lap length, l_0: The overlap of two bars that passes the force from one to the other: l_0 = α_6 l_{b,rqd} , with α_6 = 1.5 when all bars lap at one section; about 54φ in C30/37.

[glossary-lever-arm-z] Glossary: lever arm, z
lever arm, z: The distance between the tension steel and the centre of compression: z = d[0.5 + √(0.25 − K/1.134)], not more than 0.95d.

[glossary-net-load] Glossary: net load
net load: What is left on the toe or the heel when the pressure from below is set against the loads from above. Upwards on the toe, downwards on the heel.

[glossary-passive-pressure] Glossary: passive pressure
passive pressure: The largest sideways pressure a soil can offer when it is pushed: K_p γz. It needs movement to develop, so a design should not rely on all of it.

[glossary-p-h-p-v] Glossary: P_h , P_v
P_h , P_v: The horizontal and vertical components of the thrust of a sloping backfill: P cos θ at H/3 and P sin θ at the heel end.

[glossary-quasi-permanent-load] Glossary: quasi-permanent load
quasi-permanent load: The load that acts most of the time: the permanent actions plus ψ_2 = 0.3 of the surcharge. Crack control uses the steel stress under it.

[glossary-secondary-steel] Glossary: secondary steel
secondary steel: Bars at right angles to the main bars, along the wall: at least 25 % of the vertical steel of the stem and not less than A_{s,min} . Also called distribution steel.

[glossary-sloping-backfill] Glossary: sloping backfill
sloping backfill: A backfill whose surface rises behind the wall at θ. K_a is larger, the thrust acts parallel to the surface, and the virtual back is taller by a_2 tan θ.

[glossary-starter-bars] Glossary: starter bars
starter bars: Bars cast into the base and standing out of it, to which the stem bars are lapped after the base is concreted.

[glossary-str] Glossary: STR
STR: The limit state of strength of the structure, used for the members: 1.35 on permanent actions, 1.5 on variable actions.

[glossary-submerged-unit-weight] Glossary: submerged unit weight, γ′
submerged unit weight, γ′: γ_{sat} − γ_w : the weight with which soil below the water table presses on itself; 10.19 kN/m³ for γ_{sat} = 20 kN/m³.

[glossary-table-7-3n] Glossary: Table 7.3N
Table 7.3N: The table of EC2 that gives the largest bar spacing for a steel stress: 300 mm at 160 N/mm² down to 50 mm at 360 N/mm², for a crack width of 0.3 mm.

[glossary-tension-face] Glossary: tension face
tension face: The face of a member that stretches when it bends: the earth face of the stem, the bottom of the toe, the top of the heel. The main bars go there.

[glossary-tip-load-p-v-d] Glossary: tip load, P_{v,d}
tip load, P_{v,d}: 1.35P_v : the vertical component of a sloping thrust, acting as a line load at the end of the heel cantilever.

[glossary-uplift] Glossary: uplift
uplift: The upward water pressure under the base when water stands in the backfill; it reduces the vertical load and so the friction.

[glossary-water-table] Glossary: water table
water table: The level below which the soil is saturated. Below it the wall carries the submerged soil pressure and the full water pressure.

[glossary-wedge] Glossary: wedge
wedge: The triangle of soil that stands on the heel above the level of the top of the stem when the backfill slopes: a weight in the table and a triangular load on the heel.

[glossary-weep-hole] Glossary: weep hole
weep hole: A drain through the stem that lets the water behind the wall out.
